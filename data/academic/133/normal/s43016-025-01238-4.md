# China’s animal-protein-rich diets are increasingly reliant on Brazil’s land and water resources

Received: 27 September 2024

Accepted: 2 September 2025

Published online: 15 October 2025

☑ Check for updates

C. Govoni $ ^{1} $ , L. Zhuo $ ^{2,3} $ , D. M. Marchioni $ ^{4} $  & M. C. Rulli $ ^{1} $ 

China’s shift towards greater consumption of animal proteins drove substantial changes in global agricultural dynamics and particularly affected Brazil, a leading exporter of animal feed proteins. Here we examine the environmental consequences of the Sino-Brazilian soybean trade by focusing on land and water resources and the associated deforestation risk. We estimate that Brazil supplies 10% of total protein and 24–29% of animal proteins in the Chinese diet. China’s reliance on Brazilian soybeans to feed its livestock corresponded to 17.8 Mha of virtually imported Brazilian land in 2020. Irrigation is minimal, while rainwater represents an important share of virtual water trade. Although direct deforestation linked to soybean production decreased, indirect deforestation persists and soybean cultivation keeps expanding—often displacing other land uses. These findings underscore the complex interplay between global dietary shifts and environmental burdens. China’s increasing demand for animal proteins drives Brazil’s agricultural expansion and exacerbates environmental pressures in vulnerable ecosystems.

The growing demand of developed and developing countries for agricultural products has pushed the expansion of commercial agriculture in developing countries, among which Brazil stands out due to its vast reserves of natural resources and its ability to produce primary goods $ ^{1} $ . Brazil has become a leading exporter of agricultural products such as soybean, beef, sugar, poultry and coffee. Its agricultural sector is responsible for more than 20% of the country's GDP, producing approximately 36% of the world's soybeans and 13% of beef in 2021 $ ^{2} $ . Conversely, among countries in the global food market, China is now the top user and importer of animal feed crops such as soybeans and of animal-based products $ ^{3} $ . The Chinese population has increased its consumption of animal-based foods in recent decades; this growth is closely related to income growth $ ^{4} $ . China is host to almost half of the pigs raised worldwide, more than 6 billion chickens are slaughtered there each year, and the country has a large cattle herd that produces both beef and dairy products for approximately 1.5 billion inhabitants $ ^{2} $ . China needed more than 115 Mt of soybeans in 2021 to satisfy the protein demand of its livestock sector and thus of its population $ ^{2} $ . More than half of this Chinese domestic demand is met by Brazilian soybean production. Moreover, China (mainland) and Hong Kong are the largest export markets for Brazilian beef, offal and live cattle, purchasing 30% of Brazil's exports by volume in 2021 $ ^{2} $ .



The expansion of soybean cultivation, together with increasing demand for other crops, transformed Brazil from a food importer in the 1960s to one of the major exporters in the 2000s $ ^{6} $ . The rapid expansion of soybeans began in the South Region and then expanded to the Central-West Region in the 1980s due to the development of cultivars that were well adapted to the low latitudes of the tropical region $ ^{7} $ . Soybean production grew from 200,000 t in 1961 to 20 Mt in 1990 and 135 Mt in 2021, covering a harvest area of 39 Mha (ref. 8).

Thus, in addition to the United States, Brazil became a leader in the sector. International trade in soybeans started growing when China liberalized imports to meet the protein demand for animal feed use, mainly for its monogastric and edible oils sectors $ ^{9} $ . In parallel, US policies allowed American farmers to increase soybean production, while Brazil's soybean supply growth was primarily driven by the expansion of agricultural activities in the inland Cerrado region $ ^{9} $ . China imported even more Brazilian soybeans after imposing a 25% tariff on US soybeans in 2018 $ ^{9,10} $ . This trade relationship across the world has coupled human and natural systems and has implications within and between Brazil and China. This global coupling has become known as the integrated framework of telecoupling $ ^{11} $ . As China imports increasing quantities of agricultural commodities from Brazil, Chinese financing and investments are increasingly influencing and accelerating infrastructure development projects, particularly in the Amazonian region. One notable example is the planned railway connecting Mato Grosso and Pará to ports on the Amazon River, the aim of which is to facilitate soybean exports $ ^{12-14} $ . On the other hand, the main Amazonian rivers in Peru are currently the focus of a Chinese investment project to improve their navigability, enhancing connections with the Brazilian Amazon waterways. Additionally, several international railways are being proposed from Brazil to the Pacific coast, along with interoceanic roads $ ^{13} $ . All these factors, combined with the recent authorization of Chinese beef imports, may lead to indirect additional deforestation in Brazil, where China plays a non-marginal role.

The expansion of soybean cultivation and the growth of the livestock industrialization process did not occur without severe consequences for Brazilian land use and land cover, which continue to experience substantial permanent changes. Natural ecosystems, including primary forests, were deforested to free up space for new soybean plantations in the Legal Amazon for several years until 2005 $ ^{15,16} $ . At this time, the Soy Moratorium agreement was signed, with Brazil's major soybean trading companies committing not to purchase soybeans produced in areas deforested after 2006. Although the agreement was later extended to 2008 during renewal $ ^{16,17} $ , several loopholes were implemented to avoid the new legislation $ ^{18} $ . When Brazil's economy was stabilized at the end of the 20th century, beef production became industrialized, enabling some farmers to invest in and improve their production. Systems of intensive animal farming grew, leading to a substantial expansion in industrial farming, feedlots and confined animal feeding operations, referred to as cattle confinement in Brazil $ ^{19} $ . The Brazilian savannah, which is located primarily in the Cerrado, has been extensively converted to agriculture and pastureland over the past several decades, and the remaining areas have been transformed. In some regions, harvesting trees for firewood and charcoal further reduces tree density $ ^{20} $ . These activities have resulted in the conversion of forests or tree-grass ecosystems into landscapes dominated by grasslands and croplands $ ^{21} $ . A reduction in tree cover can considerably impact the physical properties of land and its interactions with the atmosphere $ ^{20} $ . Agricultural land-use changes have been the major driving force behind land-cover transformation in Brazil, leading to large-scale forest conversion and colonization for cattle ranching and soybean production expansion $ ^{22} $ . Fires and deforestation are the primary consequences of this intensified land use, although the Soy Moratorium reduced direct deforestation for soybean expansion. Despite these measures, Mato Grosso has become the leading soybean-producing state, positioning Brazil as a global leader in the sector alongside the United States $ ^{18,23} $ . The Amazon continues to lose its forests, which are now being replaced by extensive grazing lands for beef production. Pasturelands and savannah formations in the Cerrado biome are burned and reconverted mainly to commercial soybean plantations that support exports; however, they are also used to produce maize and other commercial crops to meet international, rather than local, food and feed demands. Furthermore, Brazilian soybean and beef are exported to China with high amounts of virtual water embedded in their production $ ^{24} $ , causing new realities of water scarcity in the country and worsening the already severe situation in areas such as the Northeast $ ^{25} $ .



Although the extent to which China is a main contributor to the deforestation risk associated with Brazil's beef exports has already been investigated and clearly reported in the subnational analysis of zu Ermgassen et al. $ ^{26} $ , the same has not been done for soybeans. Indeed, how China contributes to deforestation risk, land-use change and water use in Brazil through the import of large amounts of soybeans has not been fully evaluated. Additionally, although the increase in protein intake in China is reported by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) $ ^{2} $ , which provides a detailed import matrix of primary and processed vegetal and animal products by foreign countries, the extent to which international imports of protein-rich feed crops support protein intake via their conversion from vegetal to animal protein remains unexamined. Recently, some authors have estimated the Chinese protein gap between domestic demand and production, which places environmental pressure on exporting regions $ ^{5} $ ; however, an in-depth examination of the origin of these proteins in terms of quantity and their conversion from vegetal to animal proteins has not been undertaken because the natural resources required to produce them have only been roughly outlined. Specifically, the extent to which Brazil supports this diet transition by providing soybeans at the expense of the country's natural resources is worth exploring.

In this study, we assess the environmental consequences associated with the export of soybeans from Brazil to China from 2004 to 2020. This assessment is quantified in terms of virtual natural resources (that is, land and water) embedded in this international trade and identification of the deforestation risk associated with soybean production in each Brazilian municipality that exports soybean to China, also considering the role of transnational trading corporations (TNCs) in this process. Moreover, a subnational analysis of both the foreign and domestic trade of soybeans in the Chinese provinces is performed. An additional analysis evaluates the role of Brazil and its resources in supporting the supply of protein in the Chinese diet via soybean, both directly as food and indirectly as animal feed.

## Results

## National and subnational analysis of soybean supply and trade in Brazil and China

A net increase in the quantity of soybeans exported from Brazil was registered from 2004 to 2020; the amount grew from 36 Mt to 98 Mt. China was the main driver of this increase, with a 5-fold increase in total soybean imports and an 8-fold increase in imports from Brazil; a peak of an 11-fold increase occurred in 2018 (6 Mt in 2004, 68 Mt in 2018 and 60 Mt in 2020) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). Concurrently, a decline in Brazilian soybean exports to the European Union (EU28) was observed, dropping from 21–22 Mt in 2004–2005 to 13 Mt between 2017 and 2019, with a slight rebound to 16 Mt in 2020. Looking at the origin of soybean exports, while there was 'just' a 6-fold increase in flows of soybeans from the Amazon region to other countries (excluding China), a more than 100-fold increase was registered in the flows to China, almost all of these coming from municipalities in the Amazon biome of Mato Grosso state (Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, despite this relevant increase, in absolute terms, in 2020 half of soybeans exported from the Amazon region are still routed to countries other than China (for example, EU28, Vietnam, Turkey, Russia), as was before 2004. However, the increases registered for the flows from the Cerrado and Mata Atlântica biome municipalities remain relevant. Savannas and the Atlantic forest are being cleared for cropland expansion, as are areas historically devoted to pasture. This situation is the result of soy production operations that have moved from the Amazon region to avoid the Soy Moratorium and zero-deforestation commitments.

## Land and water resources embedded in Brazilian soybean production and trade

When soybean exports are considered, a virtual trade of natural resources embedded in the production of this primary crop is implied. In 2020, Brazil produced soybeans on an area of 37.2 Mha of land, of which 29.8 Mha was virtually exported to meet the demand of other countries. China virtually imported 17.8 Mha of Brazilian land cultivated with soybeans, whereas, in 2004, only 3 Mha were imported. Similarly, the virtual land use associated with soybean exports to the EU28 decreased from 8.8 Mha in 2004 to 4.9 Mha in 2020, reflecting a declining trend in European soybean imports from Brazil. Notably, although soybeans are grown throughout the country, their agricultural productivity varies greatly across states due to weather conditions during the growing season, with water deficit being a major factor. ## Deforestation exposure associated with the Sino-Brazilian soybean system

Data on deforestation in Brazil indicate that the hectares of forest exposed to deforestation and linked to soybean production and trade represent 2–3% of the total land used to produce soybeans in the country. The total amount of land exposed ranges between 0.6 Mha (in 2020) and 1 Mha (in 2017) out of 37 Mha and 34 Mha of total soybean area in the same years, respectively. The data reveal how China's soybean imports have contributed differently to deforestation in Brazil from 2013 to 2020 $ ^{29} $ . In 2013, China imported 40% of Brazil's soybean production (32.8 Mt), accounting for only 33% (0.26 Mha) of the total estimated deforestation exposure for that year (91% of origin accuracy). By 2020, China imported 50% of Brazil's soybeans (60.8 Mt) responsible for 57% (0.33 Mha) of the total deforestation exposure (85% of origin accuracy) $ ^{29} $  (Fig. 6). Notably, deforestation exposure associated with China-imported soybeans between 2013 and 2020 was concentrated mainly in Rio Grande do Sul (37%) in the Pampa biome, followed by Tocantins, Maranhão and Piauí states (14%, 12% and 11%, respectively) in the Matopiba region (comprising the states of Maranhão, Tocantins, Piauí and Bahia). ## Brazilian support for China’s protein transition

The increase in soybean imports from Brazil and other countries has resulted from the shift in diet, mainly towards foods of animal origin, that China has been undergoing since the beginning of the century. The data reveal that the daily protein supply of the Chinese population increased from an average of 86 g per capita per day in 2004 to 122 g per capita per day in 2020, with a 57% increase in protein from livestock-based foods (from 23 to 36 g per capita per day) and from fish and aquatic products (from 7 to 11 g per capita per day). Specifically, we estimated a 75% increase in the protein supply from fish and other aquatic products from aquaculture and a 155% increase in the protein supply from foods from livestock-intensive systems. Although the soybean food supply increased only slightly and its direct consumption remains mainly related to the refined oil (with no protein content) used for cooking, its use as animal feed for livestock industrial production and aquaculture has increased dramatically. The amount of soybeans used for feed in China rose nearly 4-fold from 2004 to 2020, accounting currently for more than 60% of the proteins used in the feed sector, in particular as dehydrated meal (46% protein content) (Fig. 7). The estimated protein efficiency, that is, the percentage of protein inputs as feed effectively converted to animal edible proteins, increased (from 23% to 30%), with the exception of recent years (since 2018), when the effect of the African swine fever affected efficiency in the pork sector.



Brazil contributed to nearly 16–17% of soybean domestic use in China in 2004, reaching a peak of 68% of total soybean use in 2018. With respect to plant protein availability and considering protein conversion efficiency from plant to animal products, Brazil generates a real global trade market of plant (direct) and animal (indirect) proteins. In fact, from 2018 to 2020, Brazil provided approximately 10% of the total protein supply for the Chinese diet and between 24% and 27% of the animal-protein supply, whereas in 2004, these values barely reached 1%. To contextualize these findings within broader dietary patterns, the same analysis for the EU28 reveals that while Brazil's contribution to China's protein supply has grown notably over time, its role in the European diet has remained relatively stable. In the EU28, Brazilian soybean imports contributed to approximately 7–12% of animal-protein consumption and 12–17% of total protein supply, showing a slight decreasing trend over the years (Supplementary Fig. 5).

## Discussion

Humans and natural systems worldwide are increasingly connected through distant processes, with implications for global challenges such as food security, water security and environmental protection $ ^{30} $ . Often, domestic pressure on food production due to population and income growth has in many countries been 'outsourced' to foreign countries $ ^{31} $ . The concept of telecoupling refers to these socioeconomic and environmental interactions over distances $ ^{30} $ . The international food trade has a great influence on global food production and, consequently, on natural resources. Brazil and China constitute an emblematic example of a telecoupled system, as further evidenced by this study.

The last two decades have seen Brazil increase in crop production through the expansion and intensification of agriculture, leading the country to become one of the world's major food exporters. Around 30% of the increase in soybean output has come from yield gains driven by mechanization, agrochemical use and integrated pest management $ ^{32} $ . A key intensification strategy is double cropping, in particular soybean followed by second-season corn (the so-called 'safrinha'), which now accounts for 77% of Brazil's corn production, mainly in the Cerrado $ ^{8} $ . This practice boosts land productivity and optimizes water use but also raises concerns about soil degradation, agrochemical run-off and hydrological shifts $ ^{32} $ . Although intensification has helped curb cropland expansion, land expansion continues to have a greater impact on green water use for soybean production $ ^{33} $ .

During the same period, China experienced a high rate of rural-to-urban migration fluxes that, together with increasing population and income, led to an increase in food demand, especially for foods based on animal protein $ ^{1} $ . This situation has led China to increase its imports of primary goods, particularly foods from cattle (beef and meat preparations) and soybeans used as food and monogastric and dairy animal feed. Brazil's growing trade ties with China are already reshaping global agricultural markets. As US–China trade tensions persist, Brazil is capturing a larger share of China's agricultural imports, particularly in key sectors such as soybeans, meat and coffee. Although this shift is boosting Brazil's agricultural sector, it may also drive-up local food prices, fuelling domestic inflation. Beyond bilateral effects, disruptions or conflicts in Brazil–China trade could ripple through global supply chains, threatening food security worldwide.

Concerning soybeans, with comparative advantages in both the cost and price of Brazilian soybeans, China and Brazil have been increasingly interdependent in global soybean trade for over a decade $ ^{9} $ . The oligopoly-like trade pattern has uncertain risks from local to global, including trade spats, climate change impacts on yield losses, and market changes with cost and price. In January 2025, China temporarily halted soybean imports from five firms which accounted for about 30% of the total amount shipped from Brazil to China in 2024. In March 2025, China announced a 10% increase in tariff on soybeans imported from the United States $ ^{34,35} $ . Such unexpected events may cause higher production pressure and amplify the trade-offs in environmental degradations on the soybean fields for the other unsuspended exporting companies in Brazil.

With limited land, even if it maximizes yield, China can only increase national soybean production by up to 34% of the current total imports from Brazil $ ^{36} $ . The risk of soybean yield losses due to climate changes such as drought in Brazil $ ^{37} $  may cause a relevant supply gap in China or result in short-term cropping expansion in other major exporters, such as the United States and Argentina, with higher negative environmental impacts $ ^{38} $ . The global soy market and the price of soybeans are affected by global-scale events such as war or pandemics which can also cause unexpected shocks to the global trade matrices. Although China's soybean imports have recently stabilized, our findings remain highly relevant because they underscore the long-term effects of past demand growth and the associated land-use changes in Brazil. Even in a context of slower or stagnant import growth, maintaining current volumes may still pose serious risks of deforestation and environmental degradation—particularly in light of potential climate-induced yield declines, which could necessitate further land conversion to sustain supply.



At the same time, in a broader landscape of growing geopolitical uncertainty and shifting supply alliances, international trade remains a vital mechanism for redistributing agricultural production across regions and achieving more efficient use of global land and water resources. As such, trade can serve as a key instrument for advancing global sustainability, provided it is guided by strong governance, equitable policies and shared sustainability standards. New sustainable policy frameworks such as the 'Zero Deforestation by 2030' target by the Brazilian government, the 'Double Carbon' target by the Chinese government or the recently signed Brazil–China bilateral agreement 'Community with a Shared Future for a More Just World and a Sustainable Planet' are some of the strategies adopted to optimize soybean agriculture towards both higher production and multiple environmental mitigation goals $ ^{39,40} $ . Improvements in water- and land-use efficiencies throughout the supply chain of traded soybeans, with unified operational and evaluation standards, should be undertaken by all the soybean trading companies $ ^{41} $ . Slowing the growth of, or even reducing, soybean consumption demand by substituting alternative protein foods with lower environmental impacts and more resilient supply chains can also work.

Brazilian beef meat between 2015 and 2017, China presented the greatest exposure to deforestation: 15,900–23,000 ha yr $ ^{-1} $  (21.7–31.1% of all beef export-associated deforestation risk). An increase in deforestation exposure associated with beef exports in 2019 and 2020 was also confirmed by the release of updated data by Trase ('Data sources'). At the same time, the findings of this study show that despite China being responsible for the highest deforestation exposure in Brazil associated with soybean production and trade (in fact, China is the major importer and user of Brazilian soybeans), after 2017, this exposure decreased, although the flows of soybeans still increased. Thus, while deforestation exposure associated with the expansion of cattle ranching is increasing, that associated with soybeans is decreasing.

This tendency is also confirmed by a recent study $ ^{42} $  in which authors estimated that cattle ranching accounts for approximately 93% of deforestation in the Brazilian Legal Amazon, followed by crops (mainly soybeans) and mining. Furthermore, the same authors reported that deforestation associated with both beef and soybean production in the Brazilian Legal Amazon is linked mainly to the domestic market and consumption rather than international trade, despite foreign importers being responsible for the highest deforestation intensity $ ^{42} $ . Soybean production has switched from direct to indirect players by expanding its production to former cattle ranching areas and displacing pasture areas to new deforested areas, thus making cattle farming a new direct driver of deforestation $ ^{7,18,43,44} $ . However, the soybean deforestation risk reported by Trase does not include indirect land-use change where the expansion of soy drives deforestation through the displacement of cattle ranching $ ^{45} $ . As a result, the role of supply chain governance remains crucial in mitigating deforestation risks.

Although the Soy Moratorium has been effective in curbing direct deforestation linked to soybean expansion, its limitations in addressing indirect land-use change remain a concern $ ^{18,46} $ . Strengthening both regulatory frameworks and corporate accountability mechanisms, alongside ongoing trade agreements and sustainability commitments with the combined efforts of local governments, importers and major supply chain actors (for example, TNCs), will be essential to further reducing deforestation risks in global soybean supply chains.

In this context, the Chinese diet transition and increasing protein demand, translated into increasing demand for protein-rich feeds for animal-source food production, remain the main driving forces in this global interconnected framework. Some authors have proposed innovative strategies to reduce China's dependency on protein imports without exceeding environmental boundaries by optimizing the food system. Zhao et al. $ ^{5} $  investigated the implementation of several holistic food system innovation strategies, which follow national policy directives, such as technological improvements in crop and livestock production, spatial relocation, reductions in food losses and waste, and protein substitution. These strategies could reduce crop imports by 78% by 2050 compared with the BAU scenario, especially for soybeans, while reducing the virtual import of natural resources and mitigating GHG emissions $ ^{5} $ .



The disproportionate use of natural resources (mainly concerning land and water use; Figs. 4 and 5) among livestock products and vegetal crop production has been widely discussed $ ^{47-49} $ . Notably, studies on local water use and virtual water trade associated with crop production and crop trade in Brazil usually focus on BW rather than GW since the use of BW resources can be directly related to water scarcity $ ^{24} $ . However, studies have revealed that water appropriation from rainfed agriculture has consequences for the carbon and water cycles, thus making GW an essential ecohydrological resource that merits scientific investigation $ ^{50} $ . In addition, the use of a relevant amount of GW to produce feed crops implies that lower quantities are available for the growth of other human food crops, thus leading to potential severe food security issues $ ^{51} $ . The trade flow of soybeans from Brazil to foreign countries translates almost completely to GW use, thus virtually displacing GW rather than BW (Fig. 5). The import of soybeans seems to be a good method to save BW because countries to which soybeans are exported, such as China and European countries, would have to use much more BW to produce soybeans domestically due to their dependence on irrigation. However, although soybeans are grown in Brazil on low-freshwater-use plantations, they represent one of the main drivers, together with beef production, of deforestation exposure in the Amazon (Fig. 6), making their export flows unsustainable.

Recent studies have revealed how the diets of Brazilians are far from meeting the standards for a sustainable and healthy diet $ ^{52} $ . Notwithstanding the importance of public awareness and the need to implement nutrition-sensitive agriculture $ ^{53} $  in addressing this issue, specific measures such as the relocation of the use of available rainwater (GW) from feed (for international export) to fruit and vegetable (for local and domestic consumption) crop production could already improve local diets.

The dominance of a small number of TNCs in the Brazilian soybean export sector underscores their considerable influence on the environmental sustainability of global supply chains. These companies, given their economic power and control over supply networks, are in a position to shape land-use practices through sourcing decisions and market incentives. Our results indicate that while these major TNCs were historically linked to a higher-than-proportional share of deforestation risk, their recent trend toward reducing exposure to deforestation-linked soy suggests a strategic shift—possibly driven by market demands, financial incentives or sustainability commitments. However, their continued dominance in the sector also raises concerns about accountability, transparency and the effectiveness of voluntary zero-deforestation commitments. The role of TNCs in environmental governance has been widely discussed in the literature, emphasizing the potential for both positive and negative environmental impacts $ ^{54} $ . As key intermediaries in global food systems, TNCs not only respond to regulatory frameworks and market pressures but also actively shape them. Their capacity to exert control over supply chains provides opportunities for large-scale sustainability transformations, yet it also raises concerns about market consolidation and the uneven distribution of power in decision-making processes. Strengthening regulatory frameworks, increasing transparency in supply chains and enhancing independent monitoring mechanisms are crucial to ensuring that corporate commitments translate into tangible environmental benefits.

To date, many countries have reorganized their economic strategy on imports due to their growing awareness of the non-sustainability of the current approach, which requires immediate and applicable intervention at the national level. In this context, China represents a special case. A closer examination of the specific Chinese scenario reveals that nearly half of global swine production originates within the country. This is driven by the country's aim to meet domestic demand and ensure food and protein security for its large population. Historically, China has encountered certain challenges in terms of food security, exemplified by the African swine fever epidemic, which afflicted the country in 2018 and drastically reduced the pig herd size. This event highlighted the importance of developing domestic livestock production to ensure an adequate food supply for the population rather than a sufficient feed supply $ ^{55} $ . To reduce reliance on food imports and mitigate epidemic risk, China has made important investments in increasing domestic livestock production through advanced technologies and improved efficiency while increasing its reliance on feed imports. However, since 2019, China's government has taken actions to increase self-sufficiency slightly by extending the planting area of soybeans from 9.33 Mha to 10.24 Mha (+4%), with a crop production growth of 12% (from 18.09 Mt to 20.28 Mt). From a consumption perspective, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs issued guidelines in 2021 and actions in 2023 to reduce the soybean meal content for livestock diets to 13% by 2025 from the current 14.5% through replacement of soybean meal with other cash crop meals $ ^{56-58} $ . Nevertheless, this could cause other environmental issues, such as the use of more water or land to produce other cash crops, such as cotton, rapeseed, peanuts, sesame or forage grass. Furthermore, the above strategies ignore the fact that the national average soybean yield in China in 2021 (1.95 t ha $ ^{-1} $ ) was 43% lower than that in Brazil (3.44 t ha $ ^{-1} $ ) or in the United States (3.47 t ha $ ^{-1} $ ). This implies that with limited additional land and water availability and improvements in agrotechnology, there is still potential to improve soybean production by closing such a yield gap in existing soybean harvested lands with no or even low water requirements.

It should also be noted that, in recent years, scientists have emphasized the need for a transition in food systems away from carbon-and resource-intensive animal-based proteins to more sustainable, climate-friendly sources to ensure environmental sustainability and improve human health $ ^{59} $ . Thus, China has to take relevant actions to meet the needs of its large and growing population via nationally coordinated food and agricultural policies aimed at increasing the protein sourced from crops rather than from the livestock sector.



## Conclusions

The analysis highlights the relevant environmental impacts associated with the Sino-Brazilian soybean trade, emphasizing the substantial virtual land and water use involved in meeting China's growing demand for animal feed and animal-based foods. Brazil's transformation into the largest soybean exporter, driven largely by Chinese imports, has led to increased deforestation, particularly in vulnerable biomes such as the Amazon and Cerrado. Although initiatives such as the Soy Moratorium have mitigated some direct deforestation, indirect land-use changes and resource exploitation persist. Furthermore, this study underscores the critical role of Brazilian soybeans in supporting increasing protein supply in China, primarily through animal feed, which in turn exacerbates environmental pressures in Brazil. Addressing these challenges requires more stringent policies and sustainable practices to balance agricultural production with environmental conservation.

Finally, the methodological framework developed in this study is designed to be transferable across different case studies, allowing for the assessment of resource use associated with the production and trade of various commodities across different countries, making the method a valuable tool for improving the analysis of the global food system.

## Methods

## Soybean market and the Sino-Brazilian virtual land and water trade

The analysis of the Brazilian soybean market was performed for 2004–2020, on the basis of trade data on individual flows of soybeans from the producing Brazilian municipalities to the importing countries and data on the deforestation risk associated with Brazilian soybean production and trade, which were evaluated from 2013 to 2020 ('Data sources').

The use of natural resources (that is, land and water) associated with soybean production in Brazil was assessed by accounting for both production aimed at feeding the Brazilian population and its animal herds, that is, domestic quantities, and productions intended to satisfy demand in other countries, that is, exporting quantities. The soybean flows were, therefore, grouped by end-use location, including 'Domestic' (that is, Brazil), 'China', 'EU28' or 'Other countries'. To provide a more comprehensive perspective, the analysis of virtual land and water use was conducted for both China and the EU28 (that is, EU27 plus the United Kingdom, for consistency with Trase data), using the same methodology. Domestic flows are associated with natural resource use for local purposes, including possible internal trade flows among states and municipalities. In contrast, soybean exports lead to the virtual displacement of natural resources, which are virtually embedded in the commodity itself, whether exported as a whole crop or in the form of a by-product (meal, oil, sauce, paste or curd). Brazil exports soy products, mainly beans, cakes and oils, and Trase uses a mass-based commodity equivalence factor to convert cake and oil products to a standard primary product equivalent via an allocation method $ ^{60} $ . Thus, no further conversions were needed.

Local and virtual land use (LU) was evaluated with the use of annual soybean-specific agricultural yields retrieved from the IBGE database $ ^{8} $  at the municipality scale and calculated as follows:

 $$ \mathrm{LU}_{m,c}\left(\mathrm{Mha}\right)=\frac{S_{m,c}}{Y_{m}}\left\{\begin{array}{ll}{{{c=\mathrm{Brazil}}}}&{{{\mathrm{LLU}}}} \\{{{c\neq\mathrm{Brazil}}}}&{{{\mathrm{VLU}}}}\end{array}\right. $$ 

where  $ S_{m,c} $  is the soybean flow from municipality m to the country of import c, and  $ Y_{m} $  is the soybean municipal yield. When Brazil is the country of import, local use or domestic trade of soybean is implied; thus, local land use, referred to as LLU, is implied, whereas we refer to

virtual land use, VLU, for the other flows. Notably, c was not always the country of final use for soybeans for the years 2019–2020 $ ^{60} $ .

The water embedded in the soybean flows was assessed via the agrohydrological model WATNEEDS $ ^{61-63} $ , which provides a physically based and spatially explicit assessment of crop water requirements. In this study, the model was applied to Brazilian soybean plantations to quantify the split between GW (from precipitation) and BW (from irrigation). For rainfed fields, GW corresponds to the actual evapotranspiration under water-limited conditions or potential evapotranspiration when soil moisture is sufficient. In irrigated fields, BW is calculated as the additional water needed beyond GW to satisfy crop requirements. The model operates at a daily time step with a spatial resolution of 1 arcminute ( $ \sim $ 2 km). Monthly outputs of GW and BW were aggregated to annual averages at the municipal level and combined with land-use statistics and the fraction of irrigated versus rainfed areas (from the Brazilian annual statistics: 'Data sources') to compute total water volumes associated with soybean cultivation and trade (GWV and BWV). Details on the model structure and its original applications can be found in Chiarelli et al. $ ^{61} $ .

 $$ \begin{aligned}GWV_{m,c}\left(km^{3}\right)&=\left(GW_{m}\left(mm\right)\times10\times LU_{m,c}\left(ha\right)\times R_{s}\left(\%\right)\right.\\&\left.\quad+GW_{m}\left(mm\right)\times10\times LU_{m,c}\left(ha\right)\times I_{s}\left(\%\right)\right)\times10^{-9}\\ &\\ BWV_{m,c}\left(km^{3}\right)&=\left(BW_{m}\left(mm\right)\times10\times LU_{m,c}\left(ha\right)\times I_{s}\left(\%\right)\right)\times10^{-9}\\ &\\&\left\{\begin{array}{ll}c=Brazil&LGW,LBW\\c\neq Brazil&VGW,VBW\end{array}\right.\\ \end{aligned} $$ 

where GW and BW represent the water (mm) needed to grow soybeans in municipality m as outputs of the model, and R and I are the percentages of soybean area that is cultivated as rainfed or irrigated, respectively, in each state s $ ^{28} $ . When Brazil is the country of import, local GW and BW volumes are involved, that is, 'LGW' and 'LBW', whereas we refer to virtual water use, that is, 'VGW' and 'VBW', for the other flows.

Given China’s large population, to provide a more comprehensive perspective, we also evaluated the per capita, in addition to the total, land and water use associated with Brazilian soybean consumption across different regions, including Brazil (domestic consumption), the EU28 and China. This allows us to understand the real situation from another dimension, beyond total volumes. Population size has been annually retrieved from FAO $ ^{2} $ .

The results are affected by uncertainties related to the accuracy of the data and by additional uncertainties that propagate during the evaluation of land and water use (Supplementary Information).

Both the land and water results at the municipality level were aggregated according to state and biome, as shown in Figs. 3 and 4. Some states lie on more than one biome (Supplementary Table 3).

Concerning the subnational analysis of the Chinese domestic soybean trade, an annual soybean feed proportion coefficient was estimated for China on the basis of the annual national use of soybeans for animal feed purposes from the FAO Food Balance Sheet (FBS) $ ^{2} $ . Soybeans used directly as feed were considered, and processed soybeans were added because these are indirectly consumed as meal extracted during soybean oil extraction. Through this process, the total soybean feed availability was obtained. The obtained soybean feed availability was then distributed across provinces in proportion to the national livestock units per province, as has already been done in other studies $ ^{64} $ . Finally, the annual provincial trade matrix of soybeans was rebuilt on the basis of the quantification method used in other studies, a methodology was developed by Zhuo et al. $ ^{64-66} $ . Finally, on the basis of the proportion of feed used for soybean consumption in each province, the feed portion of the soybean trade was extracted. This analysis did not consider horses, camels, mules, asses or aquaculture. Although these livestock are rarely slaughtered for meat in China and mainly consume roughage, aquaculture could play a non-negligible role in soybean demand in the provinces where it is widespread.



## Protein supply and virtual protein trade

The increase in protein intake by the Chinese population over the last two decades (2004–2020) and the role played by Brazilian exports in providing such proteins were evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 6). The changes in the Chinese diet and protein supply were taken from the FBS developed by the FAO $ ^{2} $ , as were the protein contents of both the food items included in the diet and the non-food items used as animal feed, such as oilseed meals. The analysis was carried out separately but in parallel for the years 2004–2009 and 2010–2020 due to changes in the methodology used in the FAO database to evaluate the variables provided through the FBS. The same dataset and approach were used for the EU28 (that is, EU27 + the United Kingdom), considering food and feed supply and protein content over the same periods. The food items included in the diet were split into vegetal and animal products according to the FAO definitions. Many vegetal products are reported in the diet, and thus, their protein content is directly consumed by humans; however, a large number of these products are also indirectly consumed in the form of feed, providing proteins through animal products. In turn, animal products were split into livestock and aquatic products according to their origin.

Concerning aquatic products, the FishStat database developed by the FAO $ ^{67} $  was used to access data on the Chinese country profile with respect to capture and aquaculture annual production. Definitions of the specific aquatic species included in the FAOSTAT fish food commodities were used to evaluate the evolution of the share of aquaculture production (in tonnes) in the country between 2004 and 2020. For the EU28, annual data from FishStat were combined across the country profiles of all member states to obtain a comprehensive dataset. As there is currently no commercial culture of cephalopod aquaculture and molluscs are usually grown in their natural habitat without the addition of feed, these two categories of aquatic products were not accounted for in the growing demand for feed in China; however, the shares of freshwater, demersal and pelagic fish, and crustaceans from capture production were included.

Similarly, data on the evolution of the number of livestock (including pigs, chickens, cattle, goats and sheep) raised in intensive/industrial versus extensive production systems in China from 2004–2020 were obtained from the China Livestock Yearbooks 2005–2013 $ ^{68} $  and the China Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Yearbooks 2014–2021 $ ^{69} $ . The statistics of livestock numbers at the farm scale were recorded at the provincial level until 2010, after which only the number of farms at different scales was reported. A relatively stable level of the number of animals per farm at different scales was found for the years 2007–2010. The reason behind the notable changes in average animal numbers per farm is the implementation of the Instructions on Promoting the Sustainable and Healthy Development of Animal Husbandry by China's State Council since early 2007 $ ^{70} $ . Therefore, the data for the years 2011–2020 were estimated by multiplying the average number of animals per intensive or extensive farm at the national level by the corresponding number of farms and were validated to match the total number of livestock for the state per year. For the EU28, specific annual data on intensively farmed livestock by species were not available. Instead, a constant value over time was used for each species. Data on intensive pig, broiler and laying hen farming were taken from Gilbert et al. $ ^{71} $ , while for ruminants (cattle, sheep and goats), the share of grazing animals reported in Malek et al. $ ^{72} $  was used as a proxy for the proportion of animals raised extensively.

These data on the transition of animal production assumed that only a certain number of livestock, that is, those raised in industrial systems, and aquatic species, that is, those produced in aquaculture, are fed with concentrated feed. Furthermore, the parts of meat, dairy and fish products that were domestically consumed but imported

were removed because their production has led to an increase in feed demand in the exporter country. The local feeds include vegetal products such as cereals, reported as feed use in the FBS, and oilseed meals, reported in the FBS as processing use, which are then multiplied by the meal extraction factor of each specific oilseed crop. Data on the share of the processed oilseed meals used as feed were taken from the PSD (Production, Supply, and Distribution) tables in the annual GAIN reports Oilseed and Products from the US Department of Agriculture database $ ^{73} $ .

These data from the livestock and aquaculture sectors were combined to obtain the annual overall value of the protein efficiency ratio (PER) for the entire zootechnical sector. In this way, it was possible to quantify the increasing role played by soybean foods and their increasing level of inclusion in animal diets, thus indirectly providing proteins to the Chinese and European population.

Then, data on the exports of food and non-food items from Brazil to China and EU28 were taken from the detailed trade matrix reported by the FAO $ ^{2} $  and converted to their protein content to confirm and quantify the role played by Brazil in the protein revolution occurring.

## Data sources

The Transparency for Sustainable Economies initiative, Trase (www.trase.earth), which provides data on individual flows of soybean from the producing Brazilian municipality to the country of first import (2004–2018) or final destination (2019–2020), was referenced $ ^{60} $ . The latest version (v.2.6) of Trase's Brazilian soy supply chain map provides data on the soybean flows traded through maritime and non-maritime shipments between 2004 and 2018, whereas data from 2019 to 2020 cover only maritime shipments. However, non-maritime shipments accounted for 3% and 1% of soybean flows in 2019 and 2020, respectively $ ^{60} $ . The overall accuracy of the Trase data was evaluated annually by comparing the total estimated flows by Trase with those reported by the official Brazil ComexStat $ ^{74} $ . The correctness of the reported origin and destination of the soybean flows retrieved by Trase were also compared and combined with data from ComexStat to assess the quality of the data and, subsequently, the accuracy of the natural resource flows. More details on the comparison between data reported by Trase and other databases are available in Supplementary Table 1. The deforestation risk associated with Brazilian soybean production and trade was evaluated from 2013 to 2020 because data from Trase were unavailable for previous years. Details on the deforestation exposure metric and the methodology used for its assessment are available in the Trase database $ ^{29} $ .

A detailed description of the input data sources and the components of the WATNEEDS model is available in Chiarelli et al. $ ^{61} $ , as are details on the technical validation and sensitivity analysis performed on the model. Unlike that described by Chiarelli et al. $ ^{61} $ , the spatial distribution of soybean plantations was retrieved from the MapBiomas Project database $ ^{28} $ , Collection 7, where annual maps of soybean-specific cropland areas at 30-m resolution are available. These latter data were upscaled to a 2-km resolution due to computational limitations. Furthermore, because spatially distributed maps of crop-specific irrigated areas were unavailable or outdated, annual statistics on irrigated soybeans at the state level were used $ ^{28} $ .

Several databases and studies were used for data in the Chinese context:

• Provincial data on soybean production and livestock units were collected from the Chinese National Bureau of Statistics $ ^{75} $ ;

- The portion of soybean meal included in the total concentrated feed intake for each animal type was taken from the China Feed Industry Yearbook $ ^{76} $ , and no differences in the feed composition of the same type of animal species across the country were assumed;

• Livestock unit coefficients for East Asia were retrieved from a previous study $ ^{77} $ ;

• National data on the trade matrices of soybeans were collected from the FAO-reported detailed trade matrix $ ^{2} $ ;



- The numbers of livestock (including pigs, chickens, cattle, goats and sheep) raised in intensive/industrial versus extensive production systems in China until 2010 were obtained from the China Livestock Yearbooks 2005–2013 $ ^{68} $  and the China Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Yearbooks 2014–2021 $ ^{69} $ , whereas data on trends in 2011–2020 were estimated from the previous ones described above.
