# Comparative analysis of smart city scientific research trends in the USA and China

Received: 18 December 2024

Accepted: 23 July 2025

Yuan Lai $ ^{1,2} $  & Haoxiang Zhao $ ^{1} $ 

Published online: 25 August 2025

Check for updates

The integrated concept of 'smart cities' has emerged as a key focus of contemporary research, yet efforts remain fragmented, split between technology-driven and human-centered approaches. Here we leverage open data and large language models to analyze smart city research proposals funded by the National Science Foundation in the USA and the National Natural Science Foundation of China. Our findings reveal common scientific inquiries alongside divergent research trajectories shaped by national contexts, exposing a persistent tension between techno-centric priorities and human-oriented imperatives. This comparative analysis of two leading global economies provides insights into the evolution of smart city research, highlighting both shared challenges and distinct pathways. Our results reveal common ground, divergent national priorities and funding mechanisms that risk dividing the field further.

The concept of 'smart cities', which integrates technological, human and institutional dimensions, has emerged as a focal point of contemporary research $ ^{1} $ . However, scientific inquiries into this domain remain fragmented and incohesive, with a noticeable divergence between techno-driven perspectives and human-centric approaches $ ^{2-6} $ . Scientific knowledge, as manifested in research papers, books, patents and other scholarly outputs, involves the systematic organization of concepts and relationships within scientific disciplines and broader fields $ ^{7} $ . Computational tools offer powerful methods for processing and synthesizing these diverse outputs, thereby illuminating collective research interests and advancing the identification of scientific frontiers $ ^{8} $ . Existing studies have examined smart cities through various lenses, including systematic literature reviews, case studies and policy analyses, with a particular focus on technology $ ^{9} $ , engineering $ ^{10} $ , social innovations $ ^{11} $ , governmental initiatives $ ^{12} $  and commercial services $ ^{13} $ .

These studies collectively underscore smart city research as a global frontier, with the USA and China in leading positions across critical dimensions such as financial investment $ ^{14} $ , scholarly output $ ^{15} $  and practical implementation $ ^{16} $  (Supplementary Section A). A perspective based on a comparison of the USA and China raises important questions, as their distinct administrative systems may fundamentally influence the roadmap of building smart cities $ ^{17} $ . Moreover, China has emerged as a global leader, with its influence extending beyond its borders and challenging the US leadership in related industries and innovations $ ^{18} $ . So far, most findings have been derived from academic publications, pilot projects or official policy documents, providing valuable insights into the convergences and divergences between the USA and China in smart city development. For example, a comparative study of smart city initiative policy documents indicates that two countries have taken different approaches: the USA relies on a market-led, participatory governance structure, whereas China tends to propose a state-driven, hierarchical model $ ^{14} $ . Scientific research proposals offer unique insights into emerging trends, strategic priorities and innovative ideas that shape the future $ ^{19} $ . Federally or nationally funded proposals further reflect development models, policy priorities and socio-political differences between the two countries $ ^{20} $ . Examining these proposals provides a forward-looking understanding of research subjects and future directions, yet, surprisingly, there has been a lack of systematic investigation into smart cities through scientific grant proposals.



This Article investigates how national contexts shape research priorities, innovation outcomes, and implementation strategies by analyzing research proposals funded by the National Science Foundation (NSF) in the USA and the National Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC). These two agencies represent the largest national, multidisciplinary science-funding organizations in their respective countries $ ^{21,22} $ . ## Results

## Thematic structure of smart city research

The selected research proposals  $ (n = 375) $  include NSF proposals  $ (n = 179 $ , spanning 2017–2023) involving 173 principal investigators from 102 organizations across 85 counties, and NSFC proposals  $ (n = 196 $ , spanning 2015–2020) involving 195 principal investigators from 104 organizations across 33 cities. Our analysis identifies 24 common themes, which include urban theory, scientific observation, urban problem-solving, sociotechnical advancement and collaborative innovation.

The results indicate that, despite a scientific consensus on urban systems, smart city research goals are driven by diverse value propositions (Fig. 1a). Distinct from pure natural sciences, the social value proposition plays a critical role in ensuring that smart city research outcomes align with the needs and priorities of urban residents, thereby guaranteeing relevance, ethicality and impact. This perspective enables researchers to prioritize issues that are most important to society, such as sustainability, public health and social equity, while also facilitating the acquisition of funding and influencing policy decisions. Moreover, it fosters public engagement and ensures that smart city solutions are contextually appropriate, ultimately contributing to the development of cities that are more sustainable, resilient and inclusive.

When evaluated on a Pasteur's quadrant $ ^{23} $ , the results reveal a shared perspective that positions smart cities as a use-inspired basic research field (Fig. 1b). Use-inspired basic research serves as a critical domain for developing practical solutions, establishing guidelines and frameworks, and creating functioning systems $ ^{24} $ . Over time, the USA and China have developed distinct focuses on pure basic or applied research. NSFC proposals place a strong emphasis on theory development, modeling and hypothesis testing—hallmarks of pure basic research that are aimed at observing and understanding complex urban phenomena to derive foundational insights. By contrast, applied research in smart cities emphasizes the science of complex systems integration, addressing domains such as transportation, energy, water, waste management, healthcare and governance. This integration requires rigorous scientific investigation into the interactions, optimizations and innovations within and across these systems to create cohesive and sustainable urban environments. System engineering, in particular, conceptualizes cities as cyber-physical systems, defining 'smartness' on the basis of the degree of integration, real-time feedback control, automation and cooperative control $ ^{25} $ .

Consequently, NSF proposals focus on infrastructure, policymaking, prototyping, service technologies and multidisciplinary collaboration. These differing methodologies arise from variations in funding preferences and mechanisms, which shape distinct technological innovation pathways. The NSF tends to prioritize interdisciplinary research and human-centered approaches, whereas the NSFC leans toward large-scale implementation and technology-driven research.

The key scientific inquiries address the complexity, morphology, mechanisms and control of urban systems, alongside human-centric factors such as mobility, behavior and social dynamics. Technological solutions are prominently grounded in four core technologies: artificial intelligence (AI), big data, distributed computing and the Internet of Things. A comparative analysis of proposal abstracts reveals differing priorities: Chinese researchers show a stronger focus on big data, whereas US researchers put a greater emphasis on AI (Supplementary Section B), despite the real-world problems that they address possibly being consistent (for example, traffic management and emergency response). This distinction underscores the growing role of AI in advancing smart city systems and solutions $ ^{26} $ . In addition, other technologies are emerging in smart city scientific research (for example, digital twins, self-driving and unmanned aerial vehicles), driving advances in research methodologies or becoming new objects of study, particularly reflected in NSF-funded proposals.

## Disparity in research development

The smart city research-funding mechanism is characterized by a simultaneous process of diffusion and concentration. NSFC funding exhibits a stronger correlation with local economic conditions compared with its US counterpart (Fig. 3). Within the USA, research funding is significantly associated with local educational attainment (Supplementary Section C), further explaining underlying socio-economic disparities. Whereas funding intensity tends to be geographically concentrated, the scope of funding has expanded across spatial and temporal scales. In China, there is a stronger traction-and-siphoning effect, where scientific research funding is increasingly concentrated in large, developed cities (Fig. 4). These spatial patterns and temporal trajectories suggest that smart city research reflects not only cross-cultural horizontal differences but also a vertical gradient that is shaped by socio-economic contexts.

In the USA, counties with weaker economic conditions and lower funding intensity often prioritize problem-solving to address gaps in academic research related to local challenges. Conversely, Chinese cities facing similar economic and funding constraints tend to focus on developing new technologies in specific locations as pilot projects.

## Discussion

Research proposals provide insights into the latest theoretical frameworks, methodological innovations and high-risk concepts that researchers aim to explore, offering a glimpse into the cutting edge of this field (Fig. 5). A prevailing trend indicates that smart cities are evolving beyond technological advancements to include the lived experiences of their inhabitants, necessitating a deep understanding of human behavior and the social implications of smart technologies. The anticipated research impact on urban development varies between the two countries. Given the substantial disparity in urbanization levels, NSF-funded proposals in the USA emphasize a decentralized, citizen-centered model of smart city development. By contrast, NSFC-funded proposals in China are more likely to prioritize a faster, centralized urban modernization process. It remains uncertain whether competitive dynamics in smart city research funding between the two nations could lead to the emergence of divergent sociotechnical ecosystems, potentially reducing the likelihood of interoperability and standardization in global initiatives.

The ongoing tension and potential synergy between techno-driven and human-centric approaches in smart city development have long shaped both academic debates and practical challenges. This evolution—ranging from models focused on information and communication technology $ ^{27} $ , through critical reflections on technological determinism $ ^{28,29} $ , to more inclusive paradigms $ ^{30} $ —has been well documented. Our study affirms this trajectory by highlighting the growing emphasis within scientific inquiry on cultural artifacts, environmental attributes and social intelligence. In particular, we identify an emerging trend in smart city research priorities, directly addressing prior critiques regarding the insufficient attention given to the asymmetric impacts of technology diffusion on disadvantaged groups $ ^{31} $ . One compelling piece of evidence lies in the emancipatory perspective that is evident in smart city research in the USA, where 95 out of 179 NSF-funded proposals (53%) addressed issues of justice, focusing mainly on three aspects: the broader societal impacts of research (for example, enhancing urban equity), inclusive participation in the research process (for example, the engagement of minority groups) and direct attention to under-represented communities. Specifically, 32 proposals concentrated on under-represented communities, including communities of color, rural areas, ethnic minorities and vulnerable communities that are frequently affected by natural disasters. Among these, nine proposals explicitly targeted the digital divide in offline communities. Other proposals aimed to improve the well-being of under-represented population groups, such as pregnant women  $ (n=2) $ , individuals with disabilities  $ (n=4) $ , older adults  $ (n=8) $ , homeless individuals  $ (n=2) $ , gig workers  $ (n=1) $ , infants and children with developmental disabilities  $ (n=2) $ , school-aged children  $ (n=1) $ , individuals with autism  $ (n=1) $ , patients with Alzheimer's disease  $ (n=1) $ , incarcerated individuals  $ (n=1) $  and refugees  $ (n=1) $ . By contrast, only four NSFC-funded proposals explicitly tackle similar concerns, focusing on home care, cognitive impairment in the elderly, community care facilities and low-income housing issues.



We attribute this difference to two main factors. First, NSF proposals—which concentrate more on use-inspired basic research or pure applied research—likely reflect the distinct real-world challenges faced by city agencies and are shaped by profound historical, political, cultural and ideological contexts. Smart city research in the USA appears to be more influenced by emancipatory scholarship that prioritizes addressing injustices and fostering human thriving—an enduring mission in urban planning—over strict objectivity and neutrality. This contrast highlights the differing national perspectives on smart cities as a research domain, which are shaped by their respective research initiatives and scholarly priorities. Second, such a distinction may due to varying levels of integration of social science in smart city development in the two countries. For instance, ethical considerations that involve privacy, security and social equity are central to smart city research, and they often diverge across countries due to varying cultural and political factors. These differences provide valuable insights into how nations balance innovation with ethical imperatives and public acceptance.

The thematic patterns of funded proposals reflect national priorities and strategic goals, illustrating each country's trajectory in smart city development and its alignment with broader scientific inquiries. Whereas both nations converge on key technologies supporting smart cities, researchers exhibit distinct methodological preferences. NSF-funded proposals, particularly those under the Smart and Connected Communities program, prioritize human-centered and sociotechnical perspectives on smart cities $ ^{32} $ . Many of these proposals adopt participatory action research as a pragmatic approach to generate both knowledge for action and knowledge through action $ ^{33} $ . Rather than solely advancing scientific knowledge, participatory action research focuses on specific communities or social groups through initiatives such as civic data design $ ^{34} $ , data for action $ ^{35} $ , citizen science $ ^{36} $  or data literacy projects $ ^{37} $ . Notably, 41% of NSF proposals (73 out of 179) emphasize capacity-building that empowers citizens to utilize smart facilities and promote public understanding of urban technologies through education. A recent bibliometric analysis indicates that existing smart city research tends to remain at the discursive level, without deeply engaging with communities and generating any visible impact $ ^{38} $ . Our study, however, reveals an encouraging trend: guided by the strategic foresight of national funding agencies $ ^{21} $ , researchers are increasingly

Whereas this study focuses on smart city scientific research, it is important to acknowledge the broader value of urban science in uncovering common patterns and universal laws that governs cities $ ^{41} $ . Urban planning, policy formulation and operational strategies in smart cities are increasingly informed by decision science, utilizing data-driven frameworks that integrate big data, ubiquitous computing, real-time analytics, machine learning and human–computer interactions to support more responsive and responsible decisions. Understanding the value propositions that drive scientific inquiries is critical, as it ensures alignment with the needs and priorities of urban populations, enhancing relevance, ethical integrity and societal impact. Despite differences in the missions and structures of national and federal scientific agencies, our analysis identifies a shared scientific foundation alongside diverse strategic approaches to smart city research, offering valuable insights for other countries navigating this rapidly evolving field. That said, this study addresses only one aspect, as it focuses exclusively on nationally funded scientific research proposals and adopts a comparative perspective between the USA and China. Future work could broaden the scope by incorporating data from the United Kingdom, India, Japan, Singapore, South Korea and other regions to develop a more comprehensive understanding. Comparative analyses of supernatural entities and international collaborative networks such as the European Union and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations will serve as a novel lens into global smart city research trends. In addition, this study is limited by inconsistencies in the data ranges of the collected research proposals and the lack of data for evaluating their progress. To further ensure the reliability of the extracted features and labels, a more rigorous data-processing pipeline should be implemented. Finally, this study underscores the pivotal role of national funding agencies in shaping smart city scientific research, yet it is crucial to recognize the complexity of funding mechanisms, which are also shaped by external factors such as public health crises $ ^{42} $  and policy shifts $ ^{43} $ . Understanding these multifaceted interactions and their long-term impact remains a vital direction for future inquiry.

## Conclusion

This study provides a novel and nuanced perspective of smart city scientific research in two of the world's leading technological and economic powers. Nationally funded proposals not only reflect the aspirations and challenges faced by researchers but also offer early insights into future policy impacts, collaboration patterns and the strategic priorities of funding agencies such as the NSF and NSFC. The proposals reveal critical areas of focus, potential advancements and existing gaps in smart city research. A persistent tension between techno-centric and human-oriented perspectives risks fragmenting smart city research, dividing it along two distinct developmental trajectories. Whereas some convergence in research priorities points to common trends, the distinct emphases observed in the two countries may further deepen divergence in scientific inquiry and the value propositions that underpin smart city initiatives. These findings underscore the importance of fostering international collaborative research to address global urban challenges effectively.

## Methods

## Systematic research proposal review

The systematic research proposal review (SRPR) methodology offers a structured approach to analyzing research proposals, to provide critical insights into the state of the art within a given field $ ^{44} $ . Figure 5 outlines our SRPR process, which encompasses data extraction, processing and analysis. As national funding agencies of the two leading global economies, the NSF is an independent agency of the US government that supports fundamental research and education in all non-medical fields of science and engineering, and the NSFC is an organization that is directly affiliated to the China's State Council for management of the National Natural Science Fund. The databases of both agencies maintain comprehensive documentation on research proposals across various domains until now. Using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses) framework $ ^{45} $ , we constructed a dataset comprising 375 research proposals. Records were identified through keyword-based searches,

automatically screened to exclude entries with incomplete topical, spatial and temporal information, and then manually assessed for thematic relevance to ensure eligibility. In addition, we pioneered the use of large language models (LLMs) for feature extraction in analyzing the research proposals $ ^{46} $ . Following validation, a comparative analysis $ ^{47} $  was conducted to investigate thematic structures, spatial disparities and temporal trends using an integrated suite of analytical tools.

## Leveraging LLMs for feature extraction

We used an advanced LLM (ChatGPT-4o) to carry out feature extraction from the research proposals, with the entire pipeline completed in September 2024. This approach has gained increasing traction in recent literature and has demonstrated robust effectiveness in processing and analyzing complex textual data $ ^{48-50} $ . A key component of leveraging LLMs was the optimization of pre-trained models through prompt engineering. To ensure domain relevance and output quality, we carefully designed prompts that clearly defined the context, role, task and requirements. The task was conducted in two stages: feature identification and feature quantification. During feature identification, we engaged in a multi-round, iterative interaction with the LLM to refine its responses and progressively adapt it to this domain-specific task (Supplementary Section D). This process resulted in 24 features spanning two domains (scientific research and technological development) and three dimensions (objectives, methodology and outcomes), which together served as a unified framework for comparing heterogeneous research proposals. During the feature-quantification stage, the LLM was used further to annotate each of the 375 research proposals in a binary manner across the 24 identified features, producing a structured dataset that encompassed all annotations. To account for disparities in text length between the NSF and NSFC proposals, we incorporated Chinese abstracts for NSFC proposals into the analysis, enabling the LLM to accommodate the characteristics of shorter texts effectively. This adjustment ensured the consistency and accuracy of feature extraction across the two datasets. We manually validated all the LLM-generated outputs to ensure their reliability before incorporating them into the subsequent comparative analysis (Supplementary Section E).

## Comparative analysis

To integrate discrete features and classify smart city projects for comparative analysis, particularly in terms of their positioning along the spectrum of basic to applied research, we used an innovative adaptation of the four-quadrant model $ ^{23} $ . This framework proved effective in remapping and visualizing research priorities while capturing shifts in the focus of research communities over time $ ^{24} $ . The identified features of scientific research and technological development were assigned to the y-axis and x-axis variables, respectively. Scores for each quadrant were derived from quantified metrics, generating cumulative annual statistics. The results were visualized as an inscribed ellipse within the discriminant coordinates, illustrating the evolving dynamics of smart city research.

To examine how science and technology address real-world challenges in the context of smart cities, we used a Sankey diagram to visualize the flow and integration of information technology. Automated word frequency analysis was first performed, followed by manual verification and classification of popular keywords into three categories: urban science, real-world problems and urban technology. These connections were then illustrated to highlight the interplay among the domains.

To investigate the relationship between smart city research funding and local socio-economic conditions, we geolocated all proposals and aggregated them at comparable administrative levels, encompassing 85 US counties and 33 Chinese cities. A linear regression model was constructed between local gross domestic product (GDP) and total research funding, normalized within a standardized coordinate system. Within the USA, this relationship was further examined by incorporating additional demographic indicators. Socio-economic statistics for NSF-funded proposals were sourced from the US Census Bureau, whereas data for NSFC-funded proposals were obtained from the National Bureau of Statistics of China. To examine the emergence, diffusion $ ^{51} $  and network formation $ ^{52} $  of smart city research projects, we quantified total funding across all administrative units and calculated the Gini coefficients for different temporal cross-sections. Funding amounts were standardized in US dollars using the prevailing exchange rate.
