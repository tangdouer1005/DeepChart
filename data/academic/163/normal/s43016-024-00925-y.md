# Partial substitutions of animal with plant protein foods in Canadian diets have synergies and trade-offs among nutrition, health and climate outcomes

Received: 9 January 2023

Accepted: 11 January 2024

Published online: 16 February 2024

Check for updates

Olivia Auclair $ ^{1} $ , Patricia Eustachio Colombo $ ^{2,3} $ , James Milner $ ^{3,4} $  & Sergio A. Burgos $ ^{1,5,6} $ 



Dietary guidelines emphasize the consumption of plant protein foods, but the implications of replacing animal with plant sources on a combination of diet sustainability dimensions are unknown. Using a combination of data from a national nutrition survey, greenhouse gas emissions from dataFIELD and relative risks from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2017, we assess the impact of partially substituting red and processed meat or dairy with plant protein foods in Canadian self-selected diets on nutrition, health and climate outcomes. The substitutions induced minor changes to the percentage of the population below requirements for nutrients of concern, but increased calcium inadequacy by up to 14% when dairy was replaced. Replacing red and processed meat or dairy increased life expectancy by up to 8.7 months or 7.6 months, respectively. Diet-related greenhouse gas emissions decreased by up to 25% for red and processed meat and by up to 5% for dairy replacements. Co-benefits of partially substituting red and processed meat with plant protein foods among nutrition, health and climate outcomes are relevant for reshaping consumer food choices in addressing human and planetary health.

Dietary choices contribute considerably to chronic diseases and climate change. In high-income countries, this double burden is partly attributed to excessive consumption of animal-source foods and low intake of plant-based foods. Studies modelling the replacement of animal-source with plant-source foods in population-based self-selected diets have generally found co-benefits for human health and environmental sustainability $ ^{1-3} $ . However, studies have assessed diets with simultaneous changes to intakes of red meat and dairy despite differences in nutrient profiles, associations with chronic diseases and greenhouse gas emissions (GHGE) $ ^{2,4,5} $ . Moreover, many have assessed the impacts of dietary replacements on surrogate measures of diet healthfulness, such as diet quality $ ^{1,6,7} $ , rather than population-level outcomes relevant to public health (such as nutrient inadequacy and life expectancy). The separate impacts of replacing meat or dairy with plant protein foods on a combination of nutrition, health and climate outcomes in self-selected diets have not been modelled.

The newest iteration of Canada's Food Guide (CFG) encourages consuming whole grains, vegetables and fruit, and protein foods, among which plant protein foods—namely, nuts, seeds, beans, peas, lentils, tofu and fortified soy beverages—should be consumed more often $ ^{8} $ . Yet, plant protein foods comprised only 5% of Canadian adults' total protein intakes in 2015, with red and processed meat and dairy each accounting for roughly 20% (ref. 9). Furthermore, dairy contributed more than half of Canadian adults' total intake of calcium and nearly 40% of vitamin D intake, both nutrients of public health concern in Canada, but also 30% of saturated fat intake $ ^{10} $ . Despite the prominent role of animal protein foods in Canadian diets, the impact of adhering more closely to CFG by displacing animal protein sources with plant protein sources on dimensions of diet sustainability is not yet known. This study aimed to assess the implications of partial substitutions of red and processed meat or dairy with plant protein foods consistent with CFG recommendations in Canadian self-selected diets, on nutrient inadequacy, health outcomes and diet-related GHGE.

## Results

## Characteristics of observed and modelled diets

The average intake of protein foods (unadjusted for energy intake) in observed diets was 69 g d $ ^{-1} $  for red and processed meat, 231 g d $ ^{-1} $  for dairy and 38 g d $ ^{-1} $  for plant protein foods (Extended Data Table 1). Roughly half of red and processed meat was consumed as beef and veal, two-thirds of dairy was consumed as milk and half of plant protein foods was consumed as legumes. The overall consumption of red and processed meat, dairy and plant protein foods was greater for males than for females (Supplementary Table 1). After the substitutions were implemented, the intake of plant protein foods increased proportionally in the scenarios to nearly double when half of red and processed meat was replaced and to quadruple when half of dairy was substituted. The greater intake of plant protein foods in the dairy scenarios than in the red and processed meat scenarios was due to the large intake of fluid milk in observed diets, which was replaced directly by fortified soy beverage in these scenarios. Total energy intake remained relatively constant across scenarios, ranging between +18 and +39 kcal d $ ^{-1} $  from observed diets (1,878 kcal d $ ^{-1} $ ; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1,851, 1,906 kcal d $ ^{-1} $ ).



## Adherence to dietary guidance

Replacing red and processed meat or dairy with plant protein foods resulted in greater overall adherence to CFG as determined by the Healthy Eating Food Index (HEFI)-2019 (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Table 2). Relative to observed diets, the total HEFI-2019 score increased by 14% (50.2 points; 95% CI: 49.8, 50.5) when half of red and processed meat was substituted and by 12% when half of dairy was substituted (49.1 points; 95% CI: 48.8, 49.5; P < 0.0001). Gains in total HEFI-2019 scores increased similarly when one-quarter of red and processed meat or dairy was substituted (by 12% or 9%, respectively; P < 0.0001). Notably, component scores for plant-based protein foods increased by 79% when half of red and processed meat was replaced and by 95% when dairy was substituted (P < 0.0001).

Replacing red and processed meat, but not dairy, increased protein inadequacy by 0.8% to 1.9% (P<0.0001) relative to observed diets.

## Changes to diet-related GHGE

The observed diet-related GHGE of Canadian adults was 3.99 kg carbon dioxide equivalents  $ \left(\mathrm{CO}_{2}\mathrm{eq}\right) $  per person per day (95% CI: 3.88, 4.11; Table 2). The diet-related GHGE of males was 1.5-fold that of females. Diet-related GHGE decreased up to 25% when half of red and processed meat was replaced with plant protein foods (27% for males, 21% for females; P < 0.0001), but only up to 5% when half of dairy was substituted (males and females; P < 0.0001). Reductions were proportionally less when one-quarter of animal protein foods was replaced for both combined and sex-stratified data. Observed diets exceeded the per capita planetary boundary for  $ CO_{2}eq $  by 2.1-fold compared with 1.7-fold or 2.0-fold when 50% of red and processed meat or dairy was replaced, respectively.

## Discussion

The implications of dietary guidance emphasizing greater consumption of plant protein foods depend largely on the types of animal-source foods being displaced. We found that partially replacing red and processed meat led to the greatest co-benefits across dimensions of diet sustainability. In comparison, substituting dairy attenuated synergies to health outcomes and diet-related GHGE and, despite decreasing excess intakes of saturated fat, posed a trade-off with calcium inadequacy. Importantly, while replacing red and processed meat with plant proteins worked synergistically to increase life expectancy, decreases in life expectancy stemming from reducing milk intakes were compensated for by increasing intakes of plant protein foods in the dairy scenarios. Our findings suggest that the current dietary guidance in Canada can lead to substantial gains for human health and environmental sustainability if shifts to incorporate more plant protein foods coupled with reductions to red and processed meat, in particular, are implemented.

Similar to our findings, substituting meat and dairy with plant-based alternatives in self-selected diets in the Netherlands increased intakes of iron, but decreased intakes of calcium $ ^{2} $ . An advantage of assessing replacements of meat or dairy separately, however, was that we were able to parse changes to nutrient inadequacy as stemming mostly from the dairy scenarios. Likewise, replacing milk with plant-based beverage substitutes in French self-selected diets increased the probability of adequacy for bioavailable iron and decreased the probability of adequacy for calcium, but only when substitutes were unfortified $ ^{1} $ . Despite mandatory fortification of plant-based beverages with calcium in Canada, levels did not match those for all milk products (for example, powdered milk) $ ^{12} $ . Moreover, dairy products, such as cheese and yogurt, were replaced with plant protein foods with naturally low calcium contents. Therefore, as dairy products contributed more than half of total calcium intakes among Canadian adults in 2015 (ref. 10), their replacement could affect calcium status in vulnerable populations (that is, females and older adults). Although our replacements improved iron intakes, particularly for premenopausal females, non-haem iron from plants is less bioavailable than haem iron from animal products. Indeed, studies on blood biomarkers have generally shown that the prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia is highest among vegetarian females $ ^{13} $ . Trade-offs with calcium inadequacy and bioavailable iron present a challenge in meeting requirements for these nutrients from foods alone when substituting animal protein with plant protein sources. However, we found that accounting for supplements drastically reduced levels of inadequacy for calcium among females and older adults, but only slightly for iron among premenopausal females. Supplements also reduced the percentage of the population with inadequate vitamin D intakes; however, differences between observed and modelled diets were less than 1% regardless of whether intakes were from foods alone or combined with supplements. Including age- and sex-specific recommendations for supplementation in CFG, particularly for calcium, and targeting fortification of plant protein foods to align with changes to dietary guidance could be useful in ensuring that at-risk subpopulations meet requirements for these shortfall nutrients.



Replacing red and processed meat, but especially dairy, with plant protein foods greatly reduced the percentage of adults exceeding recommendations for saturated fat. Using data from the same Canadian nutrition survey, one study found that substituting animal-source foods high in saturated fat with plant-based foods could ensure that all Canadians keep their saturated fat intakes below 10% of total energy intake $ ^{14} $ . Modelling diet–disease relationships for cardiovascular outcomes using relative risks (RR) for milk and other dairy products from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) when these become available may provide a clearer understanding of the overall effects of dairy on health.

In our study, replacing red and processed meat or dairy with plant protein foods led to positive health outcomes. In particular, replacing red and processed meat instead of dairy led to greater health impacts, especially for males. Similarly, replacing 25% and 50% of red and processed meat with a mix of legumes and vegetables in a representative sample of Swedish adults resulted in 0.3 and 0.5 million fewer years of life lost over 20 years, respectively, of which gains among males were double those of females $ ^{17} $ . As in our study, the authors found that replacing milk with a plant-based substitute increased years of life lost, although an RR for the plant-based substitute was not included in the model. Therefore, the types of animal protein foods being displaced and sex will define the magnitude of overall gains to health resulting from dietary guidance emphasizing plant protein foods.

We also found that substituting red and processed meat reduced diet-related GHGE to a greater extent than did replacing dairy. Studies from North America and Europe found that substituting 25% to 50% of red meat or beef with plant proteins reduced diet-related GHGE by 10% to 29% (refs. 1,18). However, fewer studies have assessed the environmental impacts of replacing dairy with plant protein foods independently of meat and other animal protein sources. Moreover, while other studies have shown that males have a higher diet-related GHGE compared with females $ ^{7} $ , our study can attribute this finding to higher observed intakes of red and processed meat as opposed to dairy, which led to the attenuation of sex differences among diet-related GHGE in the dairy scenarios. Importantly, even the largest reductions to diet-related GHGE from substituting half of red and processed meat with plant protein foods would exceed the per capita planetary boundary. Therefore, dietary change must be coupled with complementary interventions on the part of producers and consumers, such as improving production practices and reducing food loss and waste, to avoid the most detrimental impacts of climate change.



The graded nature of our replacement scenarios showcased the degree of impacts potentially attainable from substituting partial amounts of red and processed meat or dairy with plant protein foods, as opposed to excluding them altogether. Interestingly, low-GHGE and high-nutritional-quality observed European diets did not exclude any particular animal-based foods, although they were consumed in smaller quantities relative to average diets $ ^{19} $ . Furthermore, studies have found that more Canadians reported making an effort to reduce their red meat consumption (as opposed to all meats or dairy) than those who reported following a plant-based dietary pattern $ ^{20} $ . For context, our most stringent scenario led to an increase in plant protein food intake equivalent to three-quarter cups of boiled lentils per day. As meat and dairy consumption is engrained in Western culture, partial replacements could be a culturally acceptable alternative towards more sustainable diets.

Our study had several strengths and limitations. Integrating three outcomes focused on nutrition, health and diet-related GHGE enabled us to assess the compatibility of scenarios involving two distinct groups of animal protein foods, which revealed synergies from substituting red and processed meat but trade-offs from replacing dairy. Although there are intrinsic differences in the time frames of the outcomes reported, we based our outcomes on usual intakes to reflect habitual dietary patterns $ ^{21} $ , which if maintained long-term would address these differences. We accounted for individual preferences by replacing plant protein foods by the proportions in which they were originally consumed. A limitation of our approach is that the classification scheme we used combined traditional (for example, tofu) and novel soy-based foods (for example, soy burger) despite their nutritional discrepancies. However, these foods comprised a small proportion of total plant protein foods consumed by our sample (6.8%), so changes to nutrient inadequacy that may arise from this would likely be minor. Furthermore, we used standardized RR for foods available in the GBD 2017 to model population-level health outcome responses to dietary scenarios, but these did not capture all protein foods assessed in our study. While dietary risks were chosen by the GBD collaborators based on probable or convincing evidence of a causal relationship with chronic disease, food–disease relationships are contentious, particularly for unprocessed red meat, and are subject to change as new methodological approaches and data become available $ ^{22} $ . A limitation of using RR in modelling health outcomes is the possibility for residual confounding $ ^{23} $ . We used the 95% CI of RR to provide nominal lower and upper bounds of health outcomes, but these may have overestimated the widths of the CI owing to their aggregation across diseases. Moreover, we predict that the small increases in total energy intake from observed diets across replacement scenarios of up to 39 kcal d $ ^{-1} $  would lead to 1.2 kg in weight gain over 3 years, which are not accounted for in our modelling of health outcomes $ ^{24} $ . Finally, despite our use of GHGE as the sole indicator of environmental impact, the level of granularity achieved from linking the impacts of dataFIELD commodities to over 2,000 foods reported in the nutrition survey through the use of recipe files and crossover between American and Canadian nutrient databases would be challenging from the use of alternative datasets that report additional impacts. Although beef originates from beef and dairy cattle, we used the average beef GHGE estimate from dataFIELD as it is not possible to accurately estimate the contribution of dairy to the Canadian beef supply from publicly available data. Incorporating more

environmental impacts from foods that account for specific production systems into population-level dietary analyses will be important for providing more holistic assessments.

Our study provides evidence that diets adhering more closely to the CFG recommendation to consume protein from plants more often have overall co-benefits for nutrition, health and climate outcomes, but these depend largely on the type and amount of animal protein being displaced. Replacing red and processed meat improved health outcomes and greatly reduced diet-related GHGE, for males especially, while leading to minor changes to nutrient inadequacy. Replacing dairy, on the other hand, attenuated gains to health outcomes and diet-related GHGE and, despite improvements to intakes of saturated fat, increased calcium inadequacy. Integrated assessments incorporating more sustainability dimensions (for example, economic, social) will be crucial for creating harmonized messaging that accounts for the interplay of trade-offs and synergies among sustainability dimensions to shape future consumer food choices.

## Methods

## Population sample and dietary records

We used dietary data from the 2015 Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS)—Nutrition, a cross-sectional survey representative of the population that collected information pertaining to food and beverage consumption among Canadians using 24 h dietary recalls  $ (n=20,487)^{25} $ . Primary interviews were conducted in person, with a subset of respondents (-35%) randomly selected to complete another 24 h recall by telephone 3–10 d after the first interview for the purpose of estimating usual intakes. Foods and beverages reported in the 24 h recalls  $ (n=2,621) $  were linked by Statistics Canada to their nutrient profiles from the Canadian Nutrient File $ ^{26} $ . After excluding children and adolescents  $ (n=6,568) $ , pregnant  $ (n=116) $  and breastfeeding women  $ (n=187) $ , and respondents who did not complete a 24 h recall  $ (n=4) $ , the final number of respondents was 13,612. We focused on the general adult population as they are more likely to make conscious dietary changes than children. We obtained access to Master Files of the 2015 CCHS—Nutrition by submitting a proposal (project number 20-MAPA-MCG-6679) to Statistics Canada. Ethics approval for the CCHS was granted by Statistics Canada under the Statistics Act of Canada.

## Replacement scenarios

As CFG recommends consuming protein from plants more often, we designed graded replacements (25% and 50%) of either (1) red and processed meat or (2) dairy with equal amounts (in grams) of plant protein foods. We focused our scenarios on red and processed meat and dairy as these were commonly consumed animal protein foods that contributed most to the diet-related GHGE of Canadians in 2015 (ref. 27). We replaced 25% and 50% as these quantities would be more feasible to implement by individuals than higher quantities or the exclusion of red and processed meat or dairy altogether. We substituted grams to produce more realistic scenarios as individuals typically prepare and consume foods based on weight or volume rather than calories or protein. For example, it would require two and a half times the amount of pulses to replace ground beef on an equivalent calorie basis. Red and processed meat consisted of beef and veal, lamb, pork, and luncheon and other meats (for example, liver and liver pate, offal, sausage, game meat). Dairy included milk, cheese, yogurt, cream, butter and frozen dairy. Plant protein foods comprised nuts and nut butters, seeds, legumes, and tofu and soy-based meat alternatives (for example, 'meatless' chicken). Fortified soy beverage was included in the dairy scenarios as a direct replacement of milk as it was a commonly consumed plant-based beverage in the CCHS and the only one included as an example of a plant protein food in CFG. The classification of red and processed meat, dairy and plant protein foods is shown in Supplementary Table 7.

Replacements accounted for all foods, whether consumed whole or as part of mixed dishes. To account for personal preferences, animal products were replaced in proportions originally consumed by each respondent or by the sample average for those who did not report consuming any, as done in a previous study $ ^{1} $ . For respondents who did not consume red and processed meat or dairy, no replacement was done but they were still included in the analysis. We did not account for the addition or removal of foods that may accompany reduced intakes of red and processed meat or dairy. Methodological details on modelling replacement scenarios are included in Supplementary Information.



## Adherence to CFG

Adherence to CFG recommendations was calculated using HEFI-2019, a metric consisting of foods, beverages and nutrients that is scored out of 80 points $ ^{28} $ . Higher scores indicate greater adherence to Canadian dietary guidelines. As CFG does not contain age- and sex-specific serving sizes, reference amounts (similar to the concept of a serving) were used to calculate food proportions as presented in the food guide snapshot. We calculated HEFI-2019 using the population ratio method, which uses only one 24 h recall per individual. More information on HEFI-2019 is included in Supplementary Information.

## Prevalence of inadequate or excess nutrient intakes

The percentage of the population with usual intakes below DRI $ ^{29} $  for protein and nutrients of public health concern in Canada (calcium, iron, potassium, vitamin D) $ ^{30} $  and above CFG recommendations for nutrients to limit (free sugars, saturated fat, sodium) was estimated using the National Cancer Institute (NCI) method $ ^{8} $ . The NCI method uses two non-consecutive 24 h recalls to model usual intakes $ ^{21,31} $ . All models included age, sex and nuisance variables (weekend and recall sequence) as covariates. The EAR cut-point method was used to discern the prevalence of inadequacy for protein, vitamin D and calcium, as the distribution of their requirements is symmetrical $ ^{25} $ . Inadequacy could not be determined for iron as its requirements are skewed for women of reproductive ages, nor for potassium as it has an AI but not an EAR. The contribution of supplements to nutrient intakes was not included in the main analyses as dietary guidance recommends getting nutrients primarily from foods. However, as Health Canada recommends a vitamin D supplement of 10  $ \mu $ g for individuals 51 years of age and older or those that do not consume foods containing vitamin D on a daily basis $ ^{32} $ , we conducted additional analyses for the percentage of the population below requirements for vitamin D from foods (including beverages) and supplements. The analyses were conducted for supplement users and non-users separately and then combined as recommended by Statistics Canada $ ^{33} $ . We also performed these additional analyses for calcium, iron and potassium as supplement data were available for these nutrients. More information on the NCI method and details for combining intakes for supplement users and non-users are included in Supplementary Information.

## Modelling changes to health outcomes

The IOMLIFET life table model $ ^{34} $  was used to estimate changes to life expectancy and life years as done previously $ ^{35} $ . Given age- and sex-specific mortality rates, the model estimates survival patterns in a population stemming from changes in mortality risk due to modelled dietary changes. All-cause and cause-specific mortality rates for ischaemic heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer were calculated using age- and sex-specific data for population size from Statistics Canada $ ^{36} $  and mortality data from the GBD results tool $ ^{33} $ . RR associated with intake of animal and plant protein foods for the selected diseases were taken from GBD 2017 (Supplementary Table 8) $ ^{23} $ . Separate life tables were created for males and females owing to differences in underlying mortality rates and food consumption. Changes in the intake of animal protein and plant protein foods under the modelled substitution scenarios were estimated using the NCI method to reflect

usual intakes. To estimate the sensitivity of health outcomes, lower and upper bounds were calculated using the 95% CI of the RR.

## Estimation of diet-related GHGE

Foods in the CCHS were linked to GHGE estimates from dataFIELD (version 1.0) as described elsewhere $ ^{27} $ . Briefly, dataFIELD contains environmental impacts for 332 food commodities from global life cycle assessments $ ^{37} $ . Estimates for GHGE were expressed as kgCO $ _{2} $ eq per kg of commodity food using Global Warming Potential 100 (unless specified otherwise in dataFIELD). Estimates for most commodities included impacts up to the farm gate, or up to the processing gate for some refined products (for example, flours). To link GHGE estimates for dataFIELD commodities to foods and beverages in the CCHS, we first merged dataFIELD with the US Environmental Protection Agency's Recipe Database 2005–2010, which contains foods from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey broken down into commodities. We then aggregated GHGE estimates for each food and, using food codes, linked these to foods and beverages in the 24 h recalls. As dataFIELD used milk components as proxies for dairy commodities, we instead used cradle-to-processing gate Canadian estimates for dairy products from a previous study $ ^{38} $  to link directly to dairy foods and beverages in the CCHS. To account for food losses in our GHGE calculations, we used retail and consumer loss-adjustment factors for commodities from Statistics Canada or the US Department of Agriculture, which we linked to the CCHS in the same way we did dataFIELD. The diet-related GHGE of individuals was aggregated from foods in their 24 h recalls and estimated based on usual intakes using the NCI method. To put our scenarios into the global context, we estimated the percentage of diet-related GHGE exceeding the per capita planetary boundary for  $ CO_{2}eq $  for observed and modelled diets as done previously by Hallström et al. $ ^{39} $ . Details for modelling diet-related GHGE for scenarios and estimating the percentage exceeding the planetary boundary for  $ CO_{2}eq $  are included in Supplementary Information.

## Statistical analysis

We conducted statistical analyses in SAS (SAS Institute; version 9.3) using SAS-callable SUDAAN (RTI International; version 11.0.1) or RStudio (version 2022.02.3) for life tables. We used Statistics Canada's sample and bootstrap weights to obtain nationally representative estimates and to calculate variance. Descriptive statistics were used to estimate the intake of animal protein and plant protein foods and adherence to CFG; differences between observed and modelled diets were detected using two-sided paired t-tests. Differences in diet-related GHGE and the percentage of the population below or above nutrient recommendations were detected using paired one-sided z-tests. Differences between males and females were detected using unpaired two-sided z-tests. We used the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to adjust the P values for multiple comparisons. Adjusted P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
