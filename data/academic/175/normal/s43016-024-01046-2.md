# Global school plate waste estimates highlight the need for building a sustainable food education system

Accepted: 20 August 2024

Published online: 18 September 2024

Lei Feng $ ^{ID} $  $ ^{1,2,8} $ , Ruiqi Luo $ ^{1,3,8} $ , Xiaojie Liu $ ^{ID} $  $ ^{1,3} $ , Melissa Pflugh Prescott $ ^{4} $ , Weili Li $ ^{2} $ , Jie Song $ ^{2} $  & Yi Yang $ ^{ID} $  $ ^{2,5,6,7} $ 



Food waste reduction is essential for supporting the sustainability of food systems. Wasteful behaviours are difficult to change after they have been formed, highlighting the importance of early interventions. Here we present an assessment of school plate food waste from 29 countries, and examine the environmental implications, causes, and interventions. School plate waste ranged from 4% to 46% per capita per meal and was positively correlated with country income levels. On a global scale, this waste embodies ~150 Mha of cropland and ~770 MtCO $ _{2} $ e of greenhouse gas emissions; hence, reducing school plate food waste offers potentially large environmental gains. We propose a comprehensive, multistakeholder framework centred around sustainable food education that cultivates food systems knowledge and skills, and an appreciation for nature and food labour to reduce the psychological distance between youth and their food waste. To effectively implement the framework requires the support and engagement of families, communities and the broader society beyond the confines of schools.

Reducing food loss and waste (FLW) has been widely proposed as an effective strategy for a transition to sustainable food systems $ ^{1} $ : roughly one-third of the food produced for human consumption is lost or wasted globally $ ^{2} $ . Food loss occurs at farm, storage, transport and processing stages ( $ \sim $ 14% of total food available), while food waste occurs at retail, food service and household stages ( $ \sim $ 17%) $ ^{3,4} $ . Embedded in the massive FLW annually are 8–10% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions $ ^{5} $ , 25% of global farmland and fertilizer use $ ^{6} $ , and US1 trillion of economic cost $ ^{7} $ . Thus, substantially reducing global FLW could bring enormous economic, social and environmental benefits $ ^{8} $ . Recognizing these benefits, global and national efforts have increased. The United Nations targets FLW reduction in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs; SDG 12, 'Responsible Consumption and Production'). Many countries have also put in place FLW-related programmes, policies and targets in recent years $ ^{9} $ . Notably, China has enacted a comprehensive national law to fight FLW along the entire supply chain $ ^{10} $ .



Because the environmental impacts of food products accumulate along their life cycle $ ^{3} $ , food waste at consumption stages imposes the largest environmental burden. Reducing food waste requires behavioural change $ ^{11} $ . However, breaking old lifestyle habits and forming new ones—which may not be favoured or supported by the surrounding environment—is a difficult, complicated and lengthy process $ ^{12} $ .

On the other hand, good habits developed in childhood can persist through to adulthood $ ^{13,14} $ . And the environment in which a child grows up during early and middle childhood plays a crucial role in shaping the child's health lifestyles and status during adulthood $ ^{15} $ . These factors underscore the pivotal importance of early interventions, such as through food education and environmental changes in schools. Schools provide a unique opportunity because (1) students spend most of their time in schools and (2) school environments are relatively unified in comparison to households, making it easier to implement interventions. Admittedly, behaviours are shaped not just in schools but also by broad socioeconomic, cultural and political factors, and thus it takes more than education to change behaviour. But education can be an important part of the solution, and successful behaviour change has been observed in food intervention programmes that are well designed (for example, based on sound theories), apply multiple strategies and engage multiple players from school staff to families $ ^{16} $ .

An ever-growing number of studies has focused on plate waste in schools (Supplementary Data 1). Some studies measured food leftovers in school canteens $ ^{17,18} $ ; some estimated the associated environmental impacts $ ^{19,20} $ ; and others tested the efficacy of different interventions at reducing plate waste $ ^{21,22} $ . Here we present a comprehensive assessment of this literature by quantifying school plate waste and associated carbon and land footprints at the global scale, analysing its causes and drivers, and evaluating the interventions that have been studied. Then, we propose a sustainable food education framework—which is complementary to, but more comprehensive than, existing food and nutritional education approaches—to reduce student food waste. Finally, we discuss the main implications of our assessment and knowledge gaps that require future research.

## Results

## Data distribution

We compiled 107 studies that measured student plate waste in schools via either direct weighing (88.4%) or visual assessment (11.6%; Methods). From the studies, we extracted 291 data points spanning 29 countries in Europe, Asia, America and Africa (Fig. 1a and Methods). A data point may reflect the results of only one school or averaged results of multiple schools depending on the scope of the study. Most of the data were collected in the United States (n=86), Sweden (n=37), Japan (n=23), Portugal (n=22), Brazil (n=16) and China (n=14). Relatively few data were available for Australia (n=0) and Africa (n=1 in Ethiopia and n=8 in South Africa) (Fig. 1a). Classified by educational levels, there are 42, 101, 61 and 88 data points for preschools (ages <6 years), primary schools (ages 6–11 years), secondary schools (ages 12–17 years) and universities (ages 18 years and above), respectively (Fig. 1b).



## Geographic variation in school plate waste

Our database reveals large variations in school plate waste among countries, ranging from 133 to 9 g per capita per meal or 46% to 4% of the meal in terms of waste rate (Fig. 2). On the upper end of the spectrum are the United States, Denmark, South Africa and China in terms of the amount of per capita plate waste, and South Africa, Canada, Italy, Latvia, Portugal and the United States in terms of waste rate. On the lower end of the spectrum are Japan, Philippines, Switzerland and Malaysia in terms of per capita waste, and Ethiopia, Sweden, Japan and Philippines in terms of waste rate. Notably, results for the United States and Japan—both with a large number of data points—indicate that the amount of school plate waste in highly wasteful countries could be several times larger than in other countries (133 g or 29% in the United States versus 22 g or 4% in Japan).

Food waste issues in US schools have long been widely recognized $ ^{23} $ , but surprisingly plate waste amounts in several other countries, such as South Africa and China, are of similar magnitude. Further analysis indicates that not only is the amount of school plate waste in these countries higher than that of other countries included in our database, but it is also greater than the amount of food waste from households (which includes avoidable waste such as expired food, beside plate waste) in their own country on a per capita per meal basis (Supplementary Fig. 2). For example, average school plate waste in the United States, South Africa and China amounts to 133 g, 107 g, 87 g per capita per meal, in comparison to 54 g, 36 g, 58 g of household waste in those countries. These findings highlight that greater efforts should be taken to prevent and mitigate student food waste in those countries.

First, there is a dearth of school plate waste data for low-income countries (LICs) and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs). Data are especially poor for LICs, with only one data point for Ethiopia, indicating the urgent need for more studies in these countries given their contribution of future population and food demand growth $ ^{24} $ . Given these data limitations, it is challenging to determine whether school plate waste varies with income level (Fig. 3a). When grouped by education levels, the data are more evenly spread. Regarding waste amount, it does not seem to differ significantly between education levels. Regarding waste rate, there seems to be a negative association with education level, suggesting that as we grow up, we tend to waste a smaller percentage. This is plausible. As education levels rise, schools adopt more diverse meal service models, giving students greater autonomy in their choices. For example, elementary schools typically operate a meal allocation system, whereas university cafeterias commonly employ self-service buffet-style options. Also, as students grow up, they have a better understanding of their needs, are likely in more control of what to order, and develop better muscle coordination that allows them to become more efficient with their time during meal.

## Carbon and land footprint

We estimate that the carbon footprint embedded in school plate waste is 767 MtCO $ _{2} $ e (483.0–1379.6 MtCO $ _{2} $ e), with 82% from the food supply chain and 18% from food waste disposal. The carbon footprint of school plate waste is on a par with the total GHG emissions of Germany or about twice those of the United Kingdom $ ^{26} $ . The total land footprint embedded in global school plate waste is 154 Mha (76.1–334.1 Mha), which is larger than the total area of arable land in China and Russia ( $ \sim $ 122 and  $ \sim $ 119 Mha (ref. 27)). In terms of country area, it is equivalent to the size of Nigeria, or nearly four times that of Germany, or about six times of the United Kingdom $ ^{27} $ . These results indicate considerable environmental benefits if most of the plate waste in schools could be saved on a global scale.

The six drivers that appeared most frequently were satisfaction with sensory characteristics, social norms, lunch duration, menu planning, portion size and gender (see Supplementary Discussion for further details).

A wide range of interventions within canteens can be used to prevent and reduce student plate waste, such as reducing portion size $ ^{19,21} $ , training catering staff $ ^{28} $ , regularly assessing menu composition $ ^{29} $ , improving sensory quality and going trayless $ ^{30} $  (Fig. 4b). Reducing portion size encourages students to avoid taking more food than they need and can be implemented in settings with buffets and salad bars. Some studies have also shown that altering the dish shape and size can significantly reduce individual plate waste, yielding an effectiveness rate of up to 34% (ref. 31); this reduction in waste probably stemmed from the observed reduction in the amount of food initially selected. In buffet-style university dining halls, a potential strategy for decreasing food waste is trayless dining. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated by several studies through controlled trials, with food waste reduced by 18–54% when trays were unavailable $ ^{30,32} $ .

Beyond canteens, sustainable educational programmes are key to reducing food waste and improving diet quality. Many of the education interventions are information-based (for example, posters and text message campaigns); others focus on staff and teacher training. It is important to improve staff awareness: training on plating techniques, using different sizes of utensils and allowing food quantity choice by the customer have been shown to lead the staff to help students reduce food waste $ ^{33} $ . For example, an intervention strategy that involved canteen staff in advising students to choose appropriate portions reduced food waste by 61% (ref. 34). In a Spanish primary school, a didactic intervention to inform and involve students in reducing food waste achieved a reduction of up to 43% in the intervention group $ ^{35} $ . Additionally, an experimental evaluation found that plate waste was reduced in Portugal primary schools by 57% when implementing nutrition education sessions in classrooms $ ^{36} $ . ## A sustainable food education framework

We define sustainable food education as the integration of a supportive food environment with food and nutrition education that fosters the acquisition of food skills, nutrition and food systems knowledge, and an appreciation for nature and food labour to decrease the psychological distance between youth and their food waste (Fig. 5; Supplementary Discussion). Central to our framework are school canteens, where foods are consumed by students and opportunities exist for food waste reduction. School canteens have also been the focus of previous intervention research (Fig. 4b). School canteens should provide meals that are both nutritious and tasty $ ^{28} $  and leverage information campaigns directed to students, cafeteria and school staff by means of posters, verbal persuasion and staff guidance $ ^{37} $ . They should give students sufficient meal time and give staff and students greater autonomy in meal preparations and choices $ ^{38} $ , and they should promote healthy diets. Student engagement is crucial because this has been shown to improve students' awareness $ ^{39} $  and reduce cafeteria food waste $ ^{40} $ . School canteens should also provide opportunities for food recovery, such as school share tables $ ^{41} $  or composting $ ^{40} $ , to discourage landfill food disposal and normalize the concept of recycling food.

Beyond schools, it is important to engage students' family and work with other community stakeholders. Community partners can provide cooking and food skills classes to parents to facilitate student application of their sustainable food knowledge and skills at home $ ^{43} $ . ## Discussion

Our comprehensive analyses suggest the potential for large environmental gains if student plate waste could be considerably reduced, and show substantial differences in school plate waste between countries. Notably, the differences between Japan and the United States are worth highlighting and dissecting. Socioeconomic status and culture matter. Due to limited resources and heavy reliance on food imports, Japan has long developed a culture of resource conservation, particularly concerning food. This cultural ethos is encapsulated in the concept of mottainai $ ^{47} $  ('what a waste!'). In contrast, the United States, with its abundant resources and prevalent fast-food cultures, often prioritizes convenience and accessibility over resource conservation in dietary practices $ ^{48} $ , leading to less emphasis on food waste considerations. Policies also play an important role. Japan has implemented a comprehensive set of regulations and initiatives, including the Food Recycling Act, the Basic Act on Shokuiku and the Food Loss Reduction Promotion Act $ ^{49} $ . These laws have been effectively implemented across Japan with the support of various government departments and agencies at prefecture and municipality levels $ ^{49} $ . However, the United States lacks a cohesive national strategy despite commendable federal and state-level efforts to address food waste. The contrast between the two countries underscores the importance of building cultural norms and implementing comprehensive policies in reducing food waste. Japan can serve as an exemplary model for other countries and should be studied further.



Our analyses focus on food waste, but future research should explore integrated interventions that can not only minimize food waste but also promote healthy and sustainable diets. We have also identified considerable knowledge gaps for LICs and LMICs, where more field experiments are needed to better characterize their students' behaviour. Although plate waste data are more abundant in upper-middle-income countries (UMICs) and HICs, there is a need to diversify research across varying educational levels. For instance, data in Japan are concentrated at the elementary school level, making it difficult to accurately assess the situation of other school levels. Moreover, future studies of student food waste should include detailed food compositions, which will shed more light on the environmental impacts of student food waste and might help better inform interventions (see Supplementary Discussion for more details).

Our analysis of drivers and interventions highlights the complexity of school plate waste and the need to address this problem beyond school canteens $ ^{50} $ . Eating habits, practices and attitudes are acquired primarily through schools and families, and reinforced by the community, the media and children's experiences with the physical environment $ ^{50} $ , hence our proposal of a multiagency, multilevel and multimeasure framework for sustainable food education. One critical aspect about the framework is that the stakeholders' actions can be influenced by socioeconomic disparities, which are not only present among different countries but also among the different regions within a country, or even among different school districts within a city. Thus, effective implementation of the framework requires it to be tailored to local socioeconomic environments, food cultures, dietary habits, etc. Some interventions, such as poster information and going trayless, have shown potential in reducing student plate waste (Supplementary Table 3). Additional work is required to determine their generality, study how they can be improved in different settings, and assess potential synergies or trade-offs when they are implemented together with other interventions.

Significant evidence has emerged recently that food waste by students can be substantially reduced, contributing to sustainable food systems. However, many of the effective strategies are labour intensive and go beyond the confines of schools, thus necessitating a society willing to invest in such efforts.

## Carbon and land footprinting

The land and carbon footprints of school plate waste embodied in upstream food supply were calculated based on the footprint factors of each food category, taken from a recent global study $ ^{52} $ . We categorized plate waste into 17 food groups: rice, wheat and products, maize and products, roots and tubers, bovine meat, mutton and goat meat, pig meat, poultry meat, eggs, seafoods, vegetables, fruits, milk equivalents, legumes, nuts, oils, and sugar. Our footprint boundaries were drawn from cradle to retail, and included six stages: land use change, crop production, livestock/aquaculture production, food processing, food packaging, and retail. Post-retail stages (cooking and consumer's transport, etc.) were not included in our boundaries due to their high variability among countries and relatively low contribution to the total environmental footprint $ ^{52,53} $ .

For the carbon footprint calculation, we also considered methane emissions in each country resulting from the disposal of plate waste based on the Food and Agriculture Organization's (FAO's) emissions data for national solid food waste $ ^{54} $ . The emissions reported by the FAO accounted for food waste disposed in landfills and open dumps and were estimated using the first-order decay model developed by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change $ ^{55} $ . We assumed that the emissions intensity of school plate waste was the same as that of national solid food waste; the two could differ due to differences in food composition, although the differences were expected to be small $ ^{53} $ . The environmental footprints were calculated by:

 $$ \mathrm{LU}_{\mathrm{year}}=\sum_{j}\sum_{i}\mathrm{LF}_{i}\times p_{i,j}\times\mathrm{Quantity}_{j}\times\mathrm{Pop}_{j}\times3\times365 $$ 

 $$  GHG_{year}=\sum_{j}\left(\sum_{i}CF_{i}\times p_{i,j}+MF_{j}\times28\right)\times Quantity_{j}\times Pop_{j}\times3\times365 $$ 

 $$  MF_{j}=MethaneFW_{year,j}/\left(QuantitySW_{year,j}\times f_{j}\right) $$ 

where  $ LU_{year} $  and  $ GHG_{year} $  indicate, respectively, the land and carbon footprint of total plate waste in global schools, in unit of  $ m^{2} $   $ yr^{-1} $  and  $ kgCO_{2}e $   $ yr^{-1} $ , respectively. The land footprint factor  $ LF_{i} $  and the carbon footprint factor  $ CF_{i} $  represent the land use and GHG emission per unit weight (in  $ m^{2} $   $ kg^{-1} $  and  $ kgCO_{2}e $  per kg food) for food i production.  $ P_{i,j} $  is the share of food i to total plate waste in country j (in %). Quantity j denotes the school plate waste quantity per meal per student of country j, in units of gPW per meal per capita, mainly based on data from our meta-analysis.  $ Pop_{j} $  is the total number of students from country j, obtained from the UNESCO Institute of Statistics.  $ MF_{j} $  represents the amount of methane emitted from the disposal of school plate waste in country j (in  $ kgCH_{4} $  per kg waste), with Methane $ FW_{year,j} $  being the amount of methane per year from the disposal of solid food waste (in  $ kgCH_{4} $   $ yr^{-1} $ ) by country $ ^{54} $  and Quantity $ SW_{year,j} $  being the solid waste quantity per year $ ^{56} $  (in kg waste per yr), and  $ f_{j} $  is the share of food waste in solid waste per year $ ^{56} $  (in %). We assumed that students eat three meals a day, 365 days a year (and hence exhibit a similarly wasteful behaviour when eating at home).

Food composition data  $ p_{i,j} $  are critical to the footprint analyses, but they were not available in most of the school plate waste studies obtained except for China, Denmark, Indonesia, Latvia, Malaysia, Philippines, Thailand and the United States (Supplementary Data 5). To fill the gaps for the other countries, national food waste composition and national diet data, obtained from the literature or the FAO $ ^{57} $ , were used. For countries not covered in our database, their amount of plate waste,  $ p_{i,j} $ , was assumed to be equal to the mean value for countries at the same income level.

## Uncertainty analysis

The estimation of environmental footprints of global school plate waste was based on multiple data sources and assumptions. Monte Carlo analyses were thus conducted to determine the parameter uncertainty and error diffusion. For the Monte Carlo analysis (with a model run of 10,000 times), random values are generated for most variables with probabilities following a triangular distribution with the likeliest equal to the estimated value, minimum and maximum set as -10% and +10% varied from the baseline, respectively, except for school plate waste amount and the food composition in the waste. For school plate waste amount, its random values were generated from a log-normal distribution with the logarithmic value of mean and standard deviation based on our database (Supplementary Fig. 9). For food composition, its random values were generated following the sampling rule of the triangular distribution above, except for the generation of maximum and minimum, which were set as -10% and +10%, -50% and +50%, or -100% and +100% from the baseline, depending on the different data sources.
