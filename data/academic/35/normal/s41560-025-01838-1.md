# Covariance of interphasic properties and fast chargeability of energy-dense lithium metal batteries

Hyeokjin Kwon $ ^{1} $ , Seongyeong Kim $ ^{1} $ , Jonghyun Hyun $ ^{1} $ , Ha Eun Lee $ ^{2} $ , Seong Su Kim $ ^{2} $ , Yesom Kim $ ^{1} $ , Il Ju Kim $ ^{1} $ , Kyungjae Shin $ ^{1} $ , Sejin Kim $ ^{1} $ , Changhoon Park $ ^{1} $ , Hongsin Kim $ ^{3} $ , Dongseok Shin $ ^{3} $  & Hee-Tak Kim $ ^{1,4} $ 



Lithium metal batteries can achieve high energy density, alleviating range anxiety for electric vehicles. However, the rational interphase design under fast charging conditions remains difficult. Here we explore a series of pyran-based electrolytes with various substitutional anions under a fast charging condition and observe weakly  $ Li^{+} $ -associating anions facilitating uniform lithium plating under a fast charging condition. We demonstrate lithium metal batteries achieving a 5–70% state of charge (SoC) within 12 min over 350 repeated cycles at a 4C (8.4 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ ) charging rate, as well as high-energy designs delivering projected energy densities of 386 Wh kg $ ^{-1} $  reaching a 10–80% SoC within 17 min over 180 cycles. We propose that the improved fast charging performance is in tandem with the ability of the weakly  $ Li^{+} $ associating anions to suppress inorganic species clustering within the solid–electrolyte interphase and demonstrate the potential for electrolyte advancement based on the proposed mechanism.

The electrification of transportation has resulted in rapid advancements in battery technology and the development of next-generation batteries. In particular, lithium (Li) metal batteries (LMBs) comprising thin Li foils and high-Ni LiNi $ _{x} $ Co $ _{y} $ Mn $ _{z} $ O $ _{2} $  have been actively explored. Li||LiNi $ _{x} $ Co $ _{y} $ Mn $ _{z} $ O $ _{2} $  batteries have shown promising practical capabilities and can reach an energy density of 300 Wh kg $ ^{-1} $  or higher $ ^{1} $ . This has raised expectations that they may have the potential to mitigate spatial and range anxieties in electric vehicles compared with batteries built on conventional Li-ion battery chemistry $ ^{2} $ . Recent pioneering research on liquid electrolytes for Li metal anodes—including highly concentrated electrolytes $ ^{3-5} $ , localized highly concentrated electrolytes (LHCEs) $ ^{6-8} $ , weakly coordinating solvents $ ^{9-13} $  and additives $ ^{7,8,11-18} $ —has contributed to the design of a solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) and improved the energy density and lifespan of LMBs.

In addition to extended driving range, shortened charging time is considered crucial in improving the convenience of driving electric vehicles $ ^{19-21} $ . For electric vehicle applications, an 80% state of charge (SoC) within 15 min is deemed suitable according to the guidelines of the US Department of Energy $ ^{22} $ . However, for current energy-dense LMBs, the charging time surpasses 30 min, revealing a notable gap compared with the automotive target (Fig. 1a). Recently reported LMBs in practical cell parameters (projected energy density of >300 Wh kg $ ^{-1} $ ) can achieve a lifespan of approximately 100 cycles at around 1–2C rate charging (3–6 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  charging current density $ ^{8,14,23} $ ; Fig. 1b, Supplementary Figs. 1–3 and Supplementary Tables 1–3). For the application of LMBs in electric vehicles, higher charging rates and longer lifespans are required; however, achieving this remains highly challenging owing to uneven Li deposition under fast charging conditions $ ^{16,24,25} $ .

Importantly, we found that weakly  $ Li^{+} $ -associating anions (for example,  $ AsF_{6}^{-} $ or  $ PF_{6}^{-} $ ) can suppress the clustering of the inorganic components present within the SEI, which enables fast charging of the Li anode. Both the dual-salt electrolyte and LHCE exhibited a large Li grain at 0.2C charging but pulverized Li morphology with a smaller characteristic size at 4.0C charging in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2c). ## Fast charging of Li||NCM using the pyran-based electrolytes

In search of an electrolyte formulation for fast chargeable LMBs, we designed an electrolyte based on 1.2 M lithium bis(trifluorosulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI) in tetrahydropyran (THP):FEC (6:4 by volume). The baseline formulation relies on the two key factors: (1) the formation of a LiF-enriched SEI using an FEC solvent $ ^{33,34} $ , which, owing to its neutral nature, can be less influenced by the charging rate and (2) the high reduction stability and adequate Li $ ^{+} $ solvating power of the main solvent, THP, which prevent excessive solvent decomposition and organic-rich SEI formation $ ^{35-37} $  while maintaining high ionic conductivity for fast charging (Supplementary Figs. 11–13 and Supplementary Notes 1 and 2). As shown in Fig. 3a, under 4C fast-charging conditions, LiTFSI THP:FEC shows superior performance (80% capacity retention at 53 cycles without sudden overcharging) compared with the LHCE (Fig. 2a) and slightly better performance than the dual-salt electrolyte (Fig. 2b).

## Correlation of electrolyte and interphase properties

The selected area electron diffraction (Supplementary Fig. 23) pattern and XPS (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25) verified the formation of the LiF crystallites as a dominant inorganic species within the SEI in the set of pyran-based electrolytes. Notably, cryo-TEM images and corresponding inverse fast-Fourier transform images revealed differences in the size and distribution of LiF crystals depending on the anions in the pyran-based electrolyte (Fig. 4a–h). In the case of  $ LiNO_{3} $ , LiDFOB,  $ LiBF_{4} $  and  $ LiClO_{4} $  electrolytes, larger LiF crystals were observed with  $ D_{50} $  particle sizes of 5.2 nm, 4.7 nm, 3.9 nm and 4.1 nm respectively. The SEIs in the LiDFOB and  $ LiNO_{3} $  electrolytes include sporadic dark domains, indicating their inhomogeneity. In the case of LiTFSI, LiFSI,  $ LiPF_{6} $  and  $ LiAsF_{6} $  electrolytes, relatively smaller crystals were observed with  $ D_{50} $  particle sizes of 3.9 nm, 3.2 nm, 3.0 nm and 2.9 nm, respectively. Repeated cryo-TEM experiments show, statistically, the tendency of crystal size according to anions (Supplementary Figs. 26–33). Remarkably, the LiF crystallite size shows a close relationship with the thickness of the Li deposit and fast-charging performance, which highlights the influence of the SEI structure on the Li morphology.

To gain a deeper understanding, we performed a Spearman correlation analysis (rank correlation) of the electrolyte and interphase properties for the pyran-based electrolytes using different salt anions under 4C charging. We leveraged the following 14 chemical/electrochemical properties, which have a correlation with the morphological behaviour of Li: the ratio of solvent-surrounded Li $ ^{+} $  (SSL ratio $ ^{12} $ ; Supplementary Fig. 34) obtained by molecular dynamics (MD) simulation; Li $ ^{+} $ -anion binding energy obtained by density functional theory (DFT) simulation (Supplementary Fig. 35); ionic conductivity of the electrolyte ( $ \sigma_{ion} $ ; Supplementary Fig. 36); the initial interfacial resistance of an Li||Li cell (initial  $ R_{interface} $ ; Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38); the SEI layer thickness (Fig. 4); F1s, C1s and O1s atomic ratios of the SEI layer, obtained by XPS (F%, C% and O%; Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25); the Li||Li cell overpotential and interfacial resistance of the Li||Li cell after 15 cycles at 4C ( $ \eta_{15th} $  and  $ R_{interface} $ ; Supplementary Figs. 39–41 and Supplementary Note 3); the critical current density $ ^{25,42,43} $  ( $ \mu_{crit} $ ; Supplementary Fig. 42); the crystallite size within the SEI layer (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 26–33); the thickness of the deposited Li at 4C fast charging (Fig. 3b); and the cycle performance of Li||NCM811 at 4C fast charging (Fig. 3a). Figure 5a,b shows the results of the Spearman correlation analysis for the pyran-based electrolytes, delineating the observed correlations and underlying rationale as follows:

(1) As depicted in Fig. 5c, the crystallite size showed a strong correlation with thickness of deposited Li at 4C charging (r=0.85). This suggests that the widely reported claim that an amorphous and uniform SEI promotes uniform Li plating $ ^{3,7,12} $ —typically under <1C charging—remains valid even under the 4C charging condition. More importantly, the Li morphology is more strongly correlated with the crystallite size in the SEI rather than the chemical composition in the SEI (r=+0.51 for F1s, -0.41 for C1s and -0.23 for O1s). This indicates that the morphological uniformity of the SEI is more critical than the abundance of the inorganic species within the SEI.



(2) For the pyran-based electrolytes, there is a strong correlation between the thickness of deposited Li or crystallite size in SEI with  $ J_{crit.} $  (r = -0.84 and -0.96, respectively; Fig. 5d), which indicates that a more uniform Li deposition at 4C can be obtained for an electrolyte with a higher  $ J_{crit.} $  value. Cui et al. reported that  $ J_{crit.} $  value and susceptibility to SEI breakdown is responsible for the nanoscale uniformity of the SEI $ ^{25} $ .

(3) In a pyran-based electrolyte, a higher SSL ratio or a lower (less negative) Li $ ^{+} $ -anion binding energy tends to result in a smaller crystallite size (r = -0.97 and -0.90, respectively) and lower thickness of the deposited Li (r = -0.81 and -0.78, respectively; Fig. 5e). This is distinct from the typical Li-compatible electrolytes that utilize the conventional anion-derived SEI chemistry, where a lower ratio of SSL and higher Li $ ^{+} $ -anion coordination lead to a larger characteristic size of the deposited Li $ ^{3,6,12,13} $ . Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35 show that the SSL ratio and Li $ ^{+} $  binding energy of the explored anions are consistent with those reported previously $ ^{44-46} $ .



Additionally, we describe the parameter pairs showing high correlations in Supplementary Fig. 43 and Supplementary Notes 4 and 5. In the following section, we aim to demonstrate the causative relationship between the SSL ratio and crystallite size in the SEI, showing that the SSL ratio is the factor that substantively impacts SEI crystallite size.

## Anion specificity of interphase precipitation

The byproducts of electrolyte decomposition precipitate on the electrode surface through clustering and coalescence processes over a short period of time (on the order of several to tens of nanoseconds) $ ^{3,47} $ . In this regard, Chen et al. reported that the preferential decomposition of the  $ Li^{+} $ coordinating anion leads to an amorphous SEI on the Li metal because of the lack of crystallization or clustering time $ ^{3} $  from a kinetic viewpoint. However, this mechanism cannot explain the case of the pyran-based electrolyte where the crystallite size in the SEI becomes smaller with the decreasing  $ Li^{+} $ -anion coordination (Fig. 4e). From a different perspective, we consider that the clustering and coalescence of inorganic species are also greatly influenced by the properties of the surrounding electrolyte medium $ ^{48-50} $ .

We first constructed model structures containing  $ Li^{+} $ and  $ F^{-} $ atoms in an electrolyte comprising THP solvent molecules and various Li salt molecules to validate the simulatability of the observed trend in the cryo-TEM experiment. We set the LiF concentration (-1.3 M) to be much higher than the experimental solubility of LiF in a typical organic solvent at room temperature (in the order of submillimol) $ ^{38,51} $ , which mimics the supersaturated state of LiF at the interface on the verge of SEI formation. As the system equilibrates, the  $ Li^{+} $ and  $ F^{-} $ atoms precipitate during the simulation time (Fig. 6a). Notably, the degree of cluster evolution varies depending on the anions (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). The cluster size increases in the following order:  $ AsF_{6}^{-} < PF_{6}^{-} < FSI^{-} \approx TFSI^{-} < ClO_{4}^{-} \approx BF_{4}^{-} \approx DFOB^{-} < NO_{3}^{-} $ , which is in the same order as that observed by the cryo-TEM for the increasing LiF crystallite size in the SEI (Fig. 4). Our simulation can also predict a lower LiF crystallinity when a smaller cluster is formed (Supplementary Fig. 46). This phenomenon can be explained by the nonclassical nucleation theory $ ^{52} $ ; for crystalline nuclei to form, a sufficient size of amorphous nuclei must first be formed, followed by a disorder-to-order phase transition of these nuclei.

To understand the origin of the difference in the cluster size according to the anions, we examined single coalescence events within random ensembles each containing two  $ Li_{44}F_{44} $  clusters initially separated by a distance of 2.5 nm in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 47). Figure 6c shows that the coalescence process of the clusters involves four steps, as reported previously $ ^{[53,54]} $ : clusters get close to recognizing the existence of each other, close contact occurs between clusters (that is, anion bridge formation), atomic bond formation occurs ( $ F^{-}-Li^{+}-F^{-} $  neck formation) and growth of the neck; sphericization of the merged cluster. We observe that during the coalescence of  $ Li_{44}F_{44} $  clusters, the time taken for the two particles to form the first anion bridge and achieve close contact—that is, the process of the particles approaching each other—takes the longest during the coalescence process (Fig. 6d,e), showing pronounced differences depending on the type of anion involved. In the presence of  $ AsF_{6}^{-} $ ,  $ PF_{6}^{-} $ and  $ FSI^{-} $ , the process takes over 1,268 ps, 774 ps and 466 ps on average, respectively. Whereas with  $ TFSI^{-} $ ,  $ ClO_{4}^{-} $ ,  $ BF_{4}^{-} $ ,  $ DFOB^{-} $ and  $ NO_{3}^{-} $ , close contacts are formed in just 224 ps, 249 ps, 251 ps, 344 ps and 114 ps, respectively; anions with larger cluster sizes tend to take shorter times to form close contact.

Combining MD simulation results, an anion-specific LiF clustering mechanism is proposed here to explain the observed correlations as illustrated in Fig. 6g. Anions that weakly coordinate with Li $ ^{+} $  (for example, AsF $ _{6}^{-} $  or PF $ _{6}^{-} $  anions) exhibit minimal association with Li $ ^{+} $  sites on the LiF surface (Supplementary Figs. 48 and 49). This weak association allows only Li $ ^{+} $  in the electrolyte phase to selectively associate with the F $ ^{-} $  sites of the LiF cluster surface, leading to the excess positive charges on the LiF cluster surface, as supported by Fig. 6f. Note that the phenomenon of such charged clusters (or nanoparticles) to remain stable without aggregation in the electrolyte is theorized in the field of colloids $ ^{55,56} $ ; the higher the degree of particle charging (that is, surface charge), the stronger the electrostatic repulsion during intercluster approach (Supplementary Fig. 50), allowing them to overcome the attractive van der Waals forces and remain in a smaller size.

On the other hand, as anions exhibit stronger coordination with  $ Li^{+} $ , they interact more strongly with the  $ Li^{+} $ on the LiF cluster surface, effectively neutralizing its surface charge (as illustrated in the lower scheme in Fig. 6g). MD simulations revealed that such anions (for example,  $ DFOB^{-} $ or  $ NO_{3}^{-} $ anions) contribute to the formation of weaker surface charges (Fig. 6f) and induce weaker electrostatic repulsion during intercluster approach (Supplementary Fig. 50). The absence of electrostatic repulsion allows van der Waals forces to dominate intercluster attraction, facilitating rapid coalescence and the formation of larger aggregates.

The size of LiF precipitates in solution followed the trends observed in cryo-TEM and MD simulations. (Supplementary Fig. 51 and Supplementary Note 6).

## Practical considerations

We conducted pouch cell evaluations to assess the applicability of pyran-based electrolytes to practical LMBs. We tested two formats with high-power and high-energy cell designs (Fig. 7a,b). The high-power cells were projected to have an energy density of  $ 321 \, Wh \, kg^{-1} $  (at  $ 0.2 \, C $  charge/discharge with  $ 4.25 \, V $  voltage cut-off with E/C ratio of  $ 3.0 \, g \, Ah^{-1} $ ) and took 12 min to charge to  $ 70\% \, SoC $  at a  $ 4 \, C \, CC^{-1} $  CV charging with  $ 4.1 \, V $  voltage cut-off. The high-energy cells were projected to have an energy density of  $ 386 \, Wh \, kg^{-1} $  (at  $ 0.2 \, C $  charge/discharge with  $ 4.25 \, V $  voltage cut-off with E/C ratio of  $ 2.5 \, g \, Ah^{-1} $ ) and took 17 min to charge to  $ 80\% \, SoC $  at a  $ 3 \, C \, CC^{-1} $  CV charging with  $ 4.2 \, V $  voltage cut-off, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 52 and Supplementary Table 4). In the pouch cell operation, we employed a fixture with brace plates with bolts and a silicone foam pad that can deform according to cell expansion/contraction $ ^{[57,58]} $  (Supplementary Fig. 53), exerting a pressure of approximately  $ 500 \, kPa $  in the fully charged state and approximately  $ 480 \, kPa $  in fully discharged state in the early stages of cycling (Supplementary Fig. 54). We also introduced a precharging step with a  $ 0.3 \, C $  charging rate in the  $ 0–5\% $  SoC for the high-power cell and  $ 0–10\% \, SoC $  for the high-energy cell.

The  $ LiPF_{6} $  and  $ LiAsF_{6} $  electrolytes achieve a discharge capacity of 70% retention after 269 cycles and 338 cycles, respectively, for the high-power cell and 70% retention after 60 cycles and 180 cycles, respectively, for the high-energy cell (Fig. 7a,b and Supplementary Figs. 55 and 56). It is noteworthy that the LHCE electrolyte (1.5 M LiFSI in DME:TTE) exhibits overcharging within a few cycles during 3C charging, particularly when operating high-energy cells (Supplementary Fig. 57). Meanwhile, both high voltage stability and minimized electrolyte amounts are crucial to increase the energy density of LMBs $ ^{59} $ . The pyran-based electrolyte demonstrates stable operation with a high charging cut-off voltage of 4.4 V (Supplementary Fig. 58) and lean electrolyte condition (E/C ratio of 1.3 g  $ Ah^{-1} $ , 250 cycles; Supplementary Fig. 59). The positive effect of weakly  $ Li^{+} $ -coordinating anions ( $ AsF_{6}^{-} $  and  $ PF_{6}^{-} $ ) on cycling stability under the fast-charging condition was consistently demonstrated for LHCEs based on TTE and the advanced diluent 1,1,1,3,3-pentafluorobutane (PFB $ ^{60} $ ; Supplementary Figs. 60–63).



To meet the practical requirements for potential commercialization—avoiding the adoption of potentially toxic arsenic compounds—we propose an advanced  $ LiPF_{6} $  electrolyte formulation by introducing a nonpolar and large tetraphenylborate anion  $ \left(\mathrm{BPh}_{4}^{-}\right) $ . The  $ BPh_{4}^{-} $ anion possesses a large molecular size and its negative charge is delocalized over the four phenyl groups, resulting in a less negative local electrostatic potential (Fig. 7c). Consequently, it exhibits a lower binding energy with  $ Li^{+} $ (-0.91 eV) compared with  $ PF_{6}^{-} $ and  $ AsF_{6}^{-} $ (-1.11 eV and -1.10 eV, respectively). We substituted  $ LiPF_{6} $  in the electrolyte with  $ LiBPh_{4} $ , maintaining the sum of their concentration to 0.8 M. As shown in Fig. 7d, the SSL ratio increases up to 94% owing to the weak binding affinity of  $ BPh_{4}^{-} $ to  $ Li^{+} $ . However, the ionic conductivity decreases with increasing substitution of  $ BPh_{4}^{-} $ ; this trend is presumably due to the voluminous  $ BPh_{4}^{-} $ anions occupying a substantial fraction of the solution volume and the number of solvent molecules per unit solution volume decreases accordingly (Supplementary Fig. 64). As an optimized concentration based on charging capacity and overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 64d), we formulated a model electrolyte (referred to hereafter as the  $ LiBPh_{4} $  electrolyte) by formulating 0.6 M  $ LiPF_{6} $ , 0.2 M  $ LiBPh_{4} $ , and 0.4 M LiTFSI in a THP:FEC. As shown in Fig. 7e and Supplementary Fig. 65, MD simulations mimicking clustering of two  $ Li_{44}F_{44} $  demonstrate that the  $ LiBPh_{4} $  electrolyte shows a long coalescence time (2,955 ps) and strong surface charge (+3.4 e) compared with the  $ LiPF_{6} $  electrolyte (coalescence time of 774 ps and surface charge of +1.9 e). Cryo-TEM images for the SEI layer (Fig. 7f) revealed the formation of finely distributed LiF particles with  $ D_{50} $  size of 2.3–2.9 μm, smaller than the  $ LiPF_{6} $  electrolyte (2.8–3.3 μm; Supplementary Fig. 66) and the deposited Li in the  $ LiBPh_{4} $  electrolyte exhibits larger grains (Fig. 7g) and higher density (thickness of 11.4 μm with 2.1 mAh cm $ ^{-2} $  plating) compared with the  $ LiPF_{6} $  electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 67). Notably, the high-energy cell for energy-dense applications could undergo 120 repeated charging/discharging cycles and maintained 76% of the initial capacity under the 3C fast-charging condition (Fig. 7h), projecting high practical potential for fast-charging LMBs.

## Conclusions

Here, we conducted a comprehensive study on electrolyte design for fast charging of energy-dense LMBs within the automotive target range. By evaluating various pyran-based electrolyte formulations, we demonstrated the feasibility of achieving flat and dense Li deposition under 3–4C-rate fast-charging conditions using liquid electrolytes. Our finding indicates that fast-charging performance improves with increased uniformity of inorganic species within the SEI, highlighting the importance of mitigating their clustering at the interface during fast charging. We further emphasize the critical role of electrolyte composition, particularly the choice of substitutional anion, in suppressing the clustering of inorganic species. Overall, our work is a step towards addressing the challenges of fast charging of LMBs, a key requirement for future electric vehicle applications.

## Electrolytes

The electrolyte was prepared in an Ar-filled glove box. For comparison, the following electrolytes were used: 1 M LiPF₆ EC:EMC

(1:1 v/v, Phanax), 1 M LiDFOB (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.2 M LiBF $ _{4} $  (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) in FEC (98%, TCI):DEC (anhydrous, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) (3:7 in volume), and 1.5 M LiFSI (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) in DME (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich):TTE (97%, SynQuest Labs.) For the preparation of the pyran-based electrolytes, THP (anhydrous, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiAsF $ _{6} $  (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiFSI (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiTFSI (99.95%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiClO $ _{4} $  (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiBF $ _{4} $  (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiDFOB (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiNO $ _{3} $  (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), LiPF $ _{6} $  (97%, TCI) and FEC (98%, TCI) were used. The LiBPh $ _{4} $  electrolyte was prepared using lithium tetraphenylborate tris(1,2-dimethoxyethane) adduct (98%, Alfa Aesar). To replace the DME solvent in the adduct with THP, 1.19 g of tetraphenylborate tris(1,2-dimethoxyethane) adduct (corresponding to 0.2 M) was dissolved in 10 ml of THP, followed by a vacuum drying process. After drying, the electrolyte was prepared by adding THP:FEC solvent, LiPF $ _{6} $ , and LiTFSI to match the specified concentrations.

## Li morphology and SEI characterization

Morphologies of deposited Li in various electrolytes were studied using field-emission SEM (Sirion, FEI). Li plating was conducted by charging Li |Cu cells under specified conditions. The composition analysis of the SEI layer formed on the plated Li was conducted using in situ XPS (Axis-Supra, Kratos) with an X-ray source (Al, hν = 1,486.7 eV). The sample inlet of the XPS instrument was placed in an Ar-filled glove box (<0.1 ppm of  $ O_{2} $  and  $ H_{2}O $ ), minimizing the risk of sample contamination due to atmospheric exposure. For SEM and XPS measurements, the Li-plated samples were sequentially washed with the solvent corresponding to each electrolyte, dried under vacuum and transferred to the instruments.

## Cryo-TEM characterization

The SEI layer formed on deposited Li was analysed using cryo-TEM (Krios, Thermo Fisher). For the preparation of Li samples, a TEM grid (lacey carbon-supported copper grid with 200 mesh size) plated with Li (1.0 mAh cm $ ^{-2} $ ) at the specified current density was placed in an Eppendorf microtube within an Ar-filled glove box. Immediately after taking out from the glove box, the tube was plunged into liquid  $ N_{2} $  and then shattered. The prepared TEM grid samples were loaded into a cryo-autoloader built into the instrument, ensuring that the samples remained completely cryogenic throughout the entire procedure before being transferred to the cryo-TEM instrument.

## Pouch cell preparation

High-power and high-energy cells were fabricated using the following procedure. For high-power cell, a single side-coated  $ LiNi_{0.8}Mn_{0.1}Co_{0.1}O_{2} $  cathode (active material/Super P C65/polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) ratio of 96:2:2, an active material loading of 11 mg cm $ ^{-2} $ , -2.2 mAh cm $ ^{-2} $  at 0.2C, Wellcos corporation) was coated on one side of an aluminum current collector (13  $ \mu $ m). Additionally, a 20  $ \mu $ m free-standing Li anode (Honjo Metal) was laminated onto a copper (MTI, rinsed with hydrochloric acid, acetone and deionized water in sequence) current collector. The cathode and anode were separated by a PP separator (25  $ \mu $ m, Celgard). The cathode, anode, and separator were cut to sizes of 30 mm × 50 mm, 40 mm × 60 mm and 40 mm × 60 mm, respectively, using a punching machine. For the high-energy cell, a double side-coated  $ LiNi_{0.8}Mn_{0.1}Co_{0.1}O_{2} $  (provided by LG Energy Solution, active material/Super P C65/PVDF ratio of 96:2:2, active material loading of 18 mg cm $ ^{-2} $ , -3.7 mAh cm $ ^{-2} $  at 0.2C) and two 20  $ \mu $ m free-standing Li anodes laminated onto copper current collectors were used. The cathode and anode were separated by a PP separator. The cathode, anode and separator were cut to sizes of 33 mm × 50 mm, 35 mm × 52 mm and 37 mm × 54 mm, respectively, using a punching machine. The cathode and anode were welded to Al and Ni tabs, respectively, using an ultrasonic welder (WC-TW-300SJ, Wellcos). All electrodes and separators were stacked and packaged in Al pouches, with the specified amount of liquid electrolyte added. All cells were prepared in a dry room with a dew point of -40 °C.



## Energy density calculations

The projected energy density of each cell was calculated based on a stacked cell structure, using the weight and energy values of a unit bi-cell. The areal mass of the bi-cell was determined by adding the areal weights of the double-sided cathode (including the Al current collector), two separator sheets, the double-sided Li anode (including the Cu current collector) and the liquid electrolyte. The specific energy densities were calculated by dividing the areal energy of the cell by its areal mass. The areal weights of the cell components and the cell design parameters are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

## Electrochemical analyses

Unless stated otherwise, electrochemical analyses were performed using 2032-type coin cells, each containing 50  $ \mu $ l of electrolyte. Impedance spectroscopy for ionic conductivity (SUS|SUS with PP separator) and interfacial resistance (Li||Li with PP separator, two pieces of 16  $ \mu $ l 150  $ \mu $ m Li were used) was conducted using a Biologic VSP potentiostat. The measurements covered a frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz, with a perturbation amplitude of 10 mV. The critical current density and critical overpotential in the Li||Li cell were defined as the points at which nonuniform current–voltage signals began to occur while scanning the voltage at a rate of 1 mV s $ ^{-1} $ . The fast-charging performances of all coin-type Li||NCM811 full cells (16  $ \mu $ l 40  $ \mu $ m Li foil and 12  $ \mu $ l NCM811 active material/Super P C65/PVDF ratio of 96:2:2, an active material loading of 11 mg cm $ ^{-2} $ ; Wellcos corporation) were evaluated according to the following protocol: for high-energy cells, the formation protocol involved 0.2C (4.3 V) to 1C, 0.5C (4.25 V) to 1C, 1C to 1C and 2C to 1C charge–discharge cycles, each for two cycles. For high-power cells, the formation protocol involved 0.1C (4.3 V) to 1C, 0.5C (4.2 V) to 1C, 1C to 1C and 2C to 1C charge–discharge cycles, each for two cycles. During the formation process, the 1C and 2C charge cycles followed the voltage upper cut-off in the long-term cycling process with 1C current cut-off for high-power cells and 0.3C current cut-off for high-energy cells. The long-term cycling process was conducted according to the charging C rate and followed the voltage upper cut-off in the long-term cycling process, with 1C current cut-off for high-power cells and 0.3C current cut-off for high-energy cells. Li||NCM811 pouch cells were tested under pressure using a pressure jig and 5-mm-thick silicon foam pads. The galvanostatic cycling of the cells was performed using a WBCS3000L battery tester (WonATech) at 25 °C unless stated otherwise.

## Simulations

For DFT calculations, we utilized the Materials Studio package with the  $ DMol^{3} $  module. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof gradient-corrected exchange-correlation functional was used in the simulations. We implemented a double-numerical plus-polarization version 4.4 function, and a global orbital cut-off of 4 Å was applied. We used the COSMO method to simulate the solvent environment and calculate solvation-free energy, which provides solvation-free energy under a fixed solvent dielectric constant ( $ \varepsilon_{solvent} $ ) in a diluted environment. The convergence criteria for energy, gradient and displacement were set to 0.00001 Ha, 0.002 Ha Å $ ^{-1} $  and 0.005 Å, respectively. Additionally, the tolerance for self-consistent-field cycles was set at  $ 1.0 \times 10^{-6} $  eV atom $ ^{-1} $ . MD simulations were performed using the Materials Studio package with the Forcite module. The COMPASS III force field was used, along with Ewald electrostatic and atom-based van der Waals summation methods. For solvation structure analysis, periodic cells consisting of  $ \mathrm{LiX}\left(\mathrm{X}^{-}=\mathrm{AsF}_{6},\mathrm{PF}_{6},\mathrm{FSI}^{-},\mathrm{TFSI}^{-},\mathrm{ClO}_{4}^{-},\mathrm{BF}_{4}^{-},\mathrm{FDOB}^{-}\right. $  or  $ NO_{3}^{-} $ ), LiTFSI, THP and FEC ( $ Li^{3+}:X^{-}:TFSI^{-}:THP:FEC=12:8:4:61:55 $ ) were simulated at 298 K and 1 atm. To simulate LiF precipitation, periodic cells with  $ \mathrm{LiX}\left(\mathrm{X}^{-}=\mathrm{AsF}_{6},\mathrm{PF}_{6},\mathrm{FSI}^{-},\mathrm{TFSI}^{-},\mathrm{ClO}_{4}^{-},\mathrm{BF}_{4}^{-},\mathrm{FDOB}^{-}\right. $  or  $ NO_{3}^{-} $ ), LiF and THP ( $ Li^{3+}:X^{-}:THP=280:80:200:800 $ ) were constructed. Notably,

classical MD models, being nonpolarizable, generally overestimate ion–ion Coulomb interactions in sparsely soluble ionic compounds. To address this issue, a charge scale of 0.5 was applied to ionic atoms and molecules. The higher charges assigned to Li and F atoms intensified the Coulombic forces between them, sufficiently stabilizing the amorphous LiF clusters and preventing the disorder-to-order phase transition. The cells were optimized geometrically using the Smart Algorithm with a convergence tolerance of 0.001 kcal mol $ ^{-1} $  Å $ ^{-1} $ . A 5-ns-long simulation under the NPT (constant number of particles, pressure and temperature) ensemble was conducted with a time step of 1 fs. The Nose algorithm with a Q ratio of 0.1 was used, and the configurations (snapshots) were saved every 5,000 fs. The NVT ensemble was not applied to the set of MD simulations owing to LiF-precipitation-induced volume changes.
