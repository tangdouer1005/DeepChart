As populations age globally, ensuring equitable access to essential services becomes increasingly crucial. This study examines the revealed accessibility of essential services for ageing populations across the USA. A fairness index is developed to compare revealed service accessibility between high-ageing and low-ageing Census Block Groups. Using datasets that span a range of demographic and geographic contexts, this analysis identifies variations in how ageing communities access health care, housing and food supplies. The results show that while ageing communities generally experience better accessibility, this advantage is unevenly distributed and is projected to decline over time due to shifting demographics and a growing elderly population. In addition, accessibility gaps remain within high-ageing communities, with up to 70% of such areas in some states lacking access to ageing-specific services. These findings contribute to ongoing discussions on how urban environments can respond to demographic ageing with more equitable service provision.

The global demographic is witnessing an unprecedented shift towards an ageing population. According to the World Health Organization, the number of people aged 60 years or older is projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050, up from 1 billion in 2020 $ ^{1} $ . In the USA alone, an estimated 55.8 million adults were aged 65 or older in 2020 (ref. 2). This widespread demographic trend, observed in both developed and developing countries $ ^{1} $ , is poised to have far-reaching social implications over the course of the twenty-first century.

Ageing societies face a multitude of unique challenges across various facets of human life $ ^{3} $ , with access to essential services being among the most pressing. Such access not only supports the everyday needs of older adults but also contributes to their physical $ ^{6} $ , mental $ ^{7,8} $ , cognitive $ ^{9-11} $  and neurological well-being $ ^{12,13} $ . Prior research has examined the capacity of communities to deliver basic services—such as grocery shopping $ ^{14,15} $  and access to urban green space $ ^{16,17} $ —and has documented socio-economic disparities in service availability $ ^{18-20} $ , as well as its implications for urban design and planning $ ^{21,22} $ . Most research on older adults' health considers factors across various spatial scales, often using case studies and survey-based methods $ ^{23-27} $ . However, existing studies often assess service accessibility primarily in terms of facility availability, without accounting for actual usage patterns and the barriers older adults encounter when using these services. Furthermore, many studies lack a large-scale comparative perspective, which limits the ability to systematically examine disparities between communities with differing proportions of older adults (for a literature review and problem statement, see Supplementary Information Sections 1 and 2).



While this body of research provides a basis for understanding disparities, it often overlooks how physical mobility and geographic accessibility affect quality of life in ageing populations. As a result, analyses may offer an incomplete view of the challenges older adults face in navigating their environments, especially in relation to access to health care, nutrition and social services—factors that support independence and well-being. Such limitations underscore the need for a more granular and dynamic approach to studying service accessibility among older adults. Large-scale mobile datasets offer the potential to address existing gaps in understanding service accessibility and have been applied in studies of social inequality $ ^{28-32} $  and policy design $ ^{33-35} $ . In particular, researchers have used these data to examine visitation patterns and accessibility to various urban establishments.

In this study, we examine the revealed accessibility of essential services for ageing populations across the USA. This measure is derived from facility usage and evaluates the extent to which communities with both high and low proportions of older adults meet residents' needs at the level of US Census Block Groups (CBGs). Our analysis draws on large-scale data from millions of transactions and interactions, enabling detailed quantification across multiple geographic levels. Moreover, we quantify and compare revealed accessibility between communities with higher and lower percentages of older adults, offering a view of current disparities. We also project how accessibility may evolve in response to anticipated demographic shifts. These findings contribute to understanding how urban environments are responding to the changing needs of ageing populations.

## Unequal fulfilment of demand for essential services

Motivated by the observed differences in mobility patterns between high-ageing and low-ageing communities, we examined whether residents' demands for essential services are being met in each group. We used monthly visitation data from the SafeGraph dataset for April 2019 as the primary basis for analysis, as mobility patterns are consistent across different months (Supplementary Figs. 1–4). The analysis focused on 12 categories of services aligned with National Household Travel Surveys, which are widely used in studies of urban mobility and travel behaviour. These service categories are detailed in Methods. To ensure consistency and comparability, we identified the relevant NAICS codes associated with each of the 12 categories, as shown in Supplementary Table 1 (see Supplementary Information Section 4).

We assessed the demand for each essential service within a CBG by calculating the total number of visits by residents to facilities offering that service. Supplementary Fig. 5 presents the median ratio of visits to population size across the 12 categories (Supplementary Information Section 5). Among these, three services—health care services (HCS), grocery and food supply (GFS), and housing and real estate (HRE)—appear at the top, indicating higher demand than other categories. These services are directly related to health, access to daily necessities and housing. Accordingly, we focused on these three essential services in this study.

Building on our analysis of service demand, we then examined how these demands are met by the availability of services within accessible distances, a measure referred to as revealed accessibility in this study. The index i refers to points of interest (POIs) within the accessible area, while j refers to POIs outside this area. The terms  $ \alpha_{i} $  and  $ \beta_{i} $  represent the number of visits to POIs inside and outside the radius, respectively. The primary analysis of  $ A_{k}^{S,T} $  uses a 5-mile radius to define accessibility. The rationale for this threshold, supported by prior studies and travel behaviour metrics, is discussed in Supplementary Information Section 6. To test the robustness of the findings, alternative thresholds of 7.5 miles and 10 miles were also evaluated, with results remaining consistent across these distances (Supplementary Information Sections 6 and 8).

For clarity, we define  $ A^{S,T} $  as the set of revealed accessibility values A for all CBGs of type T, and  $ A^{S} $  as the combined set for all CBGs, for each essential service S. Specifically,  $ A^{S,T} = A_{1}^{S,T} $ ,  $ A_{2}^{S,T} $ , ... represents CBGs of type T, while  $ A^{S} = A_{1}^{S,H} $ ,  $ A_{2}^{S,H} $ , ...,  $ A_{1}^{S,L} $ ,  $ A_{2}^{S,L} $ , ... aggregates values from both high-ageing (H) and low-ageing (L) CBGs. The development of the accessibility metric A enables a quantitative comparison between  $ A^{S,H} $  and  $ A^{S,L} $  for each service S, with A serving as a proxy for a CBG's capacity to meet residents' essential needs. A larger A indicates a higher utilization of nearby facilities and, by extension, greater local accessibility.

To quantify the divergence between high-ageing and low-ageing CBGs, we introduce a fairness index,  $ \Delta\bar{F} $ , defined as the normalized distribution distance between  $ A^{S,H} $  and  $ A^{S,L} $ . This index enables a more in-depth assessment of regional disparities of A for comparative analysis. ## Regional differences in A

To further investigate the factors associated with revealed accessibility, we conducted regression analyses to quantify the relationship between high-ageing status and A across CBGs. The model includes control variables grouped into two main categories: (1) socio-demographic variables that include the ratio of visits to population, normalized median income, percentage with a college education or higher, percentage minority and percentage of low-wage workers; and (2) built environmental variables such as land use, urban area and National Walkability Index. A categorical variable, state, is also included in country-wide models to account for geographical and jurisdictional variation.

The country-wide results from the ordinary least-squares regression model are presented in Table 1. The regression coefficients  $ (\beta) $  for high-ageing CBGs are 0.051 (95% CI, (0.042, 0.060); P < 0.001) for HCS, 0.027 ((0.019, 0.036), P < 0.001) for GFS and 0.060 ((0.051, 0.069), P < 0.001) for HRE. These positive coefficients suggest that, at the national level, high-ageing communities are associated with higher A to all three service types than low-ageing communities, consistent with the patterns shown in Fig. 3. To assess the robustness of these results, we replicated the analysis using an expanded accessibility radius of 10 miles, and the findings remained consistent (see Supplementary Information Section 8 for details).

Variation across different US states indicates that the association between an ageing population and revealed service accessibility is regionally dependent. Figure 4a–c shows that, in most states, A for the three essential services in high-ageing CBGs is either higher than or comparable to that in low-ageing CBGs. FL demonstrates a consistently stronger positive association between ageing demographics and revealed accessibility in HCS ( $ \beta = 0.101 $  (0.086, 0.117), P < 0.001), GFS ( $ \beta = 0.079 $  (0.065, 0.092), P < 0.001) and HRE ( $ \beta = 0.091 $  (0.077, 0.106), P < 0.001). Virginia and Washington, though having fewer high-ageing CBGs, also show patterns favouring high-ageing communities, particularly in GFS (Virginia:  $ \beta = 0.103 $  (0.043, 0.163), P < 0.001; Washington:  $ \beta = 0.103 $  (0.028, 0.178), P = 0.007) and HRE (Virginia:  $ \beta = 0.089 $  (0.028, 0.149), P = 0.004; Washington:  $ \beta = 0.121 $  (0.044, 0.198), P = 0.002). In FL, this trend may reflect long-standing infrastructure and policy efforts targeted at populations. For example, the State Plan on Aging 2022–2025, led by the Florida Department of Elder Affairs, prioritizes the expansion of elder-focused services, ensuring affordable housing and improving access to health care and community-based support systems $ ^{36} $ .

In contrast, other states exhibit a more neutral or mixed association. For instance, CA and AZ both show positive associations between ageing and revealed accessibility for HRE services, but less consistent patterns for HCS and GFS. In CA, the coefficients for HCS are close to zero ( $ \beta = 0.026 (-0.003, 0.056), P = 0.077 $ ), suggesting a minimal and insignificant difference in accessibility between high-ageing and low-ageing communities. In AZ, the association between ageing and GFS is comparatively weak and statistically insignificant ( $ \beta = -0.021 (-0.049, 0.006), P = 0.124 $ ), indicating that increased retirement populations do not necessarily correspond to higher accessibility in that domain. These regional variations suggest that while state-level ageing initiatives, such as California's Master Plan for Aging $ ^{37} $ , may aim to improve service access, their effects can differ across service types or geographic areas.

## Projection analysis

However, ongoing demographic shifts towards ageing populations are expected to continue both in the USA and globally. According to projections from the US Census Bureau, individuals aged 65 and older are projected to comprise 21% of the US population by 2030 and 24% by 2060 (ref. 38). These anticipated changes motivate the need to examine how future demographic patterns may influence accessibility outcomes and to assess whether existing infrastructure and services are positioned to accommodate evolving population needs.

## Discussion

This study has some limitations that warrant further examination. The analyses are conducted at the CBG level, consistent with prior research using similar data sources $ ^{39,40} $ . This choice, while largely dependent on the availability of the datasets, offers the advantage of aggregated data that effectively identify broader patterns and trends across larger populations. Also, the visitation patterns reported in our datasets may not perfectly reflect actual service utilization, as not all visits result in meaningful or completed use of services. The findings in this study could thus be calibrated by individual-level data, enhancing our understanding of service accessibility for the ageing population. Additionally, the projection analysis in this study is based solely on demographic shifts and assumes homogeneity across CBGs. Although this provides a useful approximation, it does not account for other important drivers of change, such as migration flows (Supplementary Information Section 11), changes in service provision or demographic heterogeneity. Future research could incorporate these factors to yield a more comprehensive understanding of how ageing trends may influence accessibility. Finally, this study does not investigate causal relationships between the potential influencing factors and the two key indicators developed. While the patterns observed are informative, further investigation is needed to identify the drivers of accessibility outcomes in ageing communities and to establish causal links. This line of inquiry will be critical for informing the design of age-friendly environments that effectively support older adults' service needs.

Nevertheless, this study offers several contributions. First, it quantifies the capacity of communities to support older residents in accessing essential services. Through the comparative analysis of  $ A_{k}^{S,T} $ , we identified variations in revealed accessibility across three main service categories—HCS, GFS and HRE. By employing revealed accessibility metrics, this study enables direct assessment of how effectively different communities serve the needs of ageing populations. These findings not only provide a baseline for current service accessibility levels but also help identify areas where improvements may be needed to enhance support for older adults.

This analysis aligns with and advances the World Health Organization’s age-friendly cities framework $ ^{41} $ , which emphasizes the development of environments that support quality of life for older adults. While previous critiques have noted the framework’s limited inclusion of measurable indicators of ‘age-friendliness’ $ ^{42,43} $ , this work introduces a quantitative method to assess and potentially enhance the effectiveness of these initiatives, providing a practical tool to evaluate age-friendly policies across diverse urban settings. This evidence-driven approach moves ageing studies forward by shifting from descriptive frameworks to data-supported assessments that can inform policy decisions and resource allocation.

In addition, we introduce the fairness index, a metric developed to quantify disparities in revealed accessibility between high-ageing and low-ageing communities across multiple geographic scales, including county, state and national levels. This metric assesses the extent to which communities are able to meet residents' basic service needs, an important consideration given growing evidence that healthy ageing is influenced by factors across the life course, including lifestyle choices $ ^{44,45} $  and features of the physical environment $ ^{46-48} $ . As research increasingly advocates for the shift from 'age-friendly' to

'longevity-ready' urban environments—those designed to support well-being across all stages of life $ ^{49} $ —this metric offers a means to assess equity in service accessibility. Its use can help ensure that urban policy studies account not just for service provision but also for equity in distribution, addressing structural disparities that impact vulnerable ageing populations.

This study also provides a comprehensive analysis of both spatial diversity and temporal changes in how communities across the USA meet the service needs of ageing populations. Spatially, high-ageing communities generally exhibit higher revealed accessibility to essential services than low-ageing communities, though this pattern varies substantially across states and counties. For instance, high-ageing communities in FL consistently demonstrate higher accessibility, while in other states the differences are less pronounced or even reversed. Ageing-specific services show a more uneven spatial distribution, with notable accessibility gaps among high-ageing CBGs. Nationally, approximately half of high-ageing CBGs lack access to ageing-specific services within a 5-mile radius, and the proportion reaches up to 70% in states such as AZ. These disparities highlight potential challenges for older adults who may face mobility limitations, particularly in areas without accessible support services. The uneven distribution of ageing-specific services may reflect broader systemic issues, including differences in policy implementation, resource allocation and local planning capacity in response to population ageing.

Temporally, community dynamics are expected to shift alongside the projected increase in the ageing population in the USA. While some high-ageing communities currently exhibit relatively higher levels of accessibility, these advantages may diminish over time due to increasing demand and potential resource competition. The declining trends in  $ \Delta F $  observed in Fig. 7 suggest that even neighbourhoods currently considered ageing-friendly may face reduced accessibility over time. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing monitoring and adaptive planning strategies to sustain service accessibility amid demographic change. This forward-looking approach supports public health goals by identifying areas at risk of declining accessibility and encouraging proactive, rather than reactive, responses in policy and infrastructure development.

This study offers several actionable insights for urban planning and policymaking. First, the observed disparities in revealed accessibility suggest the need for targeted, state-specific strategies. For instance, increased investment in senior housing and health-care facilities in states such as New Jersey and Texas may address local accessibility gaps, reflecting approaches already observed in states such as FL and CA $ ^{36,37,50} $ . Second, the temporal analysis highlights the importance of anticipatory planning to accommodate the evolving needs of ageing populations, particularly with a growing share of older residents. The quantitative framework presented in this study may complement initiatives such as the World Health Organization's age-friendly cities model, which has been critiqued for lacking measurable indicators of 'age-friendliness' $ ^{41-43} $ . Finally, the fairness index developed here offers a means to identify underserved areas and inform equitable resource allocation. It may be used alongside existing analytical tools such as the Intelligible Fair City Planner (IF-City), which aims to visualize and mitigate urban inequalities $ ^{51} $ . Together, these data-driven approaches can contribute to the development of more inclusive and responsive urban environments.

## Methods

## Datasets

In this study, we leveraged several datasets to examine the mobility and visitation patterns of the ageing population in the USA. The primary dataset is SafeGraph's POIs Visitation Patterns dataset. This dataset provides detailed information on foot traffic to millions of POIs across the USA, including metrics such as the number of unique visitors, the median dwell time and the demographic composition of visitors. These data enable the analysis of the types of facilities frequently visited, visit duration and differences in mobility patterns across population groups.



The second data source is SafeGraph's Social Distancing dataset, which provides detailed measures of mobility at the CBG level. This dataset includes metrics such as travel distances from home, median time spent at home and a visits dictionary that records the number of visits to destination CBGs. Each record is linked to a unique GEOID for geographical identification. These measures enable the analysis of spatial mobility patterns, including the number of block groups visited, which serves as an indicator of spatial range and activity engagement. This dataset supports a fine-grained assessment of how ageing populations navigate their environments and helps identify disparities in access to essential services.

The third dataset comprises demographic information aggregated at the CBG level, sourced from the US Census Bureau (2019). It includes each CBG's unique GEOID, total population, percentage of the population aged 65 and older and centroid coordinates. Additionally, the dataset provides geometric boundary data, an urban area classification indicator, median household income, the percentage of residents with a college degree or higher and the percentage of non-white residents. We used these variables to characterize the socio-demographic and spatial context of each CBG in the analysis.

The fourth dataset is Version 3 of the Smart Location Database, provided by the US Environmental Protection Agency. This dataset contains built environment and employment-related indicators at the CBG level and was used to derive several key variables in the regression analyses (Table 1). These variables include land use, percentage of low-wage workers and the National Walkability Index. 'Percentage of low-wage workers' refers to the share of total workers earning US$1,250 or less per month. The National Walkability Index incorporates multiple built-environment factors that influence pedestrian activity, including intersection density, proximity to public transit and employment density within each CBG.

The data used in this study span the period from 1 April to 30 April 2019, covering all CBGs in the USA. This temporal and spatial scope enables analysis of mobility patterns at a granular level. To assess the robustness of using a single-month snapshot, we conducted additional analyses with data from other months in 2019 and found that weekly mobility patterns remained consistent (Supplementary Figs. 1–4). We thus consider the April data to be representative of broader mobility trends during the year. All mobility-related datasets used in this study are fully anonymized and aggregated by the data provider (for example, SafeGraph), with information reported exclusively at the CBG level. No personally identifiable information is included, ensuring the protection of individual privacy.

## V isitation to POIs

We used the primary datasets to analyse the visitation patterns to 12 categories of essential services, as identified through POIs and corresponding NAICS codes provided by SafeGraph. Each POI is linked to a specific service category via its NAICS code, allowing for the classification and aggregation of visitation data. The NAICS codes associated with each service type are tabulated in Supplementary Table 1. We also report the median ratios of visits to the population for each service in Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 5).

## Mobility and visitation patterns

Revealed accessibility ( $ A_{i}^{S,T} $ ). To compute  $ A_{i}^{S,T} $ , we first merged the visitation data with demographic and geographic information from the US Census Bureau. This allowed us to estimate the number of visits from each CBG to various types of POIs, which we used as proxies for the demand for the corresponding essential services. We then identified each CBG's accessible facilities by locating all POIs within a 5-mile radius of the CBG's geographical centroid. The proportion of demand

for essential service S that is met by facilities accessible to CBG i of type T is calculated using equation (1), where  $ A_{i}^{S,T} $  denotes the revealed accessibility score.

Fairness index ( $ \Delta\bar{F} $ ). To quantify disparities in revealed accessibility between high-ageing and low-ageing communities, we defined a fairness index,  $ \Delta\bar{F} $ , for a given service S. Let  $ N^{H} $  and  $ N^{L} $  denote the sizes of sets  $ A^{S,H} $  and  $ A^{S,L} $ , respectively, corresponding to the number of high-ageing and low-ageing CBGs within a given geographic unit (for example, state or county).

The fairness index is calculated as:

 $$ \Delta\bar{F}=\frac{1}{4.5}\sum_{k=1}^{9}\left(\frac{1}{N^{\mathrm{H}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{\mathrm{H}}}\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{i}^{S,\mathrm{H}}\geq\text{Percentile}\left(\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{S},\mathrm{L}},\frac{k}{10}\right)\right\}-\left(1-\frac{k}{10}\right)\right) $$ 

where Percentile  $ \left(\mathbf{A}^{\mathbf{S},\mathrm{L}},\frac{k}{10}\right) $  denotes the  $ \frac{k}{10} \times 100\% $  percentile of  $ A^{S,L} $  and

 $$ \mathbb{I}\{\mathrm{condition}\}=\left\{\begin{aligned}&1&\quad if condition holds,\\ &0&\quad otherwise.\end{aligned}\right. $$ 

This formulation allows  $ \Delta F $  to be calculated at multiple spatial scales (for example, national, state, county or city). A positive value of  $ \Delta F $  indicates that high-ageing CBGs tend to have higher accessibility than low-ageing CBGs, while a negative value reflects lower accessibility.

Projection. We estimated the projected percentage of the population aged 65 and older in each CBG for the years 2030 and 2060 using the US Census Bureau's national-level growth rate. Given the current percentage of residents aged 65 and older in CBG i, denoted  $ p_{i} $ , we projected future percentages using the following equations:

 $$ p_{i}^{2030}=(1+\bar{r}^{2030})p_{i}\mathrm{a n d}p_{i}^{2060}=(1+\bar{r}^{2060})p_{i} $$ 

where  $ \bar{r}^{2030} = \bar{p}^{2030} / \bar{p}^{2020} - 1 $  and  $ \bar{r}^{2060} = \bar{p}^{2060} / \bar{p}^{2020} - 1 $  with  $ \bar{p}^{2020} = 17\% $ ,  $ \bar{p}^{2030} = 21\% $  and  $ \bar{p}^{2060} = 24\% $  from the Census Bureau's projections $ ^{38} $ .

Due to data availability, we used 2020 values to approximate 2019 baselines. Furthermore, in analysing the projected correlation between ageing and  $ \Delta\bar{F} $ , we held the revealed accessibility  $ A_{k}^{S,T} $  constant across years. This assumption isolates the effect of demographic change by excluding potential confounding factors such as changes in demand or in the number and distribution of POIs.

Regression. To account for multiple comparisons across regression models, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate. All reported P values have been adjusted using this procedure, and statistical significance is based on the corrected values.

Policy information about studies with human participants or human data. See also policy information about sex, gender (identity/presentation), and sexual orientation and race, ethnicity and racism.

Reporting on sex and gender

Sex and gender were not considered in the study design, as the analysis was conducted at the aggregated community level using Census Block Group data. No individual-level sex or gender data were collected or analyzed. Therefore, no sex- or gender-based analyses were performed.



## Reporting on race, ethnicity, or other socially relevant groupings

Race and ethnicity data were used as control variables in regression analyses to account for community-level demographic composition. These variables were obtained from U.S. Census Bureau data and reflect aggregated, self-reported information collected through standardized census procedures. Race and ethnicity were not used as proxies for socioeconomic status or other factors. No individual-level data were analyzed. All relevant variables were included in multivariate models to account for potential confounding.

## Population characteristics

This study does not involve individual human participants. Population characteristics, including age distribution—specifically the proportion of residents aged 65 and above—were derived from U.S. Census Bureau data at the Census Block Group (CBG) level. These aggregated demographic indicators, such as age, income, education, and racial/ethnic composition, were used as covariates in the analysis. No individual-level or personally identifiable data were used.
