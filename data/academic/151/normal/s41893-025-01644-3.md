# Powering air travel with jet fuel derived from municipal solid waste

Accepted: 26 August 2025

Published online: 03 November 2025

Jingran Zhang $ ^{1,2} $ , Fang Wang $ ^{3} $ , Zhao Jia Ting $ ^{3} $ , Weiguo Dong $ ^{3} $ , Shaojun Zhang $ ^{3,4,5,6} $ , Ye Wu $ ^{3,4,5,6} $ , Chris P. Nielsen $ ^{1} $ , Ming Zhao $ ^{3,4,6,7,8} $ , Jiming Hao $ ^{3,4,5,6} $  & Michael B. McElroy $ ^{1,9} $ 



Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is a promising decarbonization solution for aviation, but its adoption remains below 1% due to high cost. As municipal solid waste (MSW) continues to grow and sustainable disposal remains challenging, converting MSW into SAF offers an attractive pathway to align the goals of zero-waste cities and carbon-neutral aviation, given its reliable availability, low emissions and low cost. Here we evaluate MSW as feedstock for SAF production via industrial-scale gasification and Fischer–Tropsch synthesis data. The life cycle assessment indicates that MSW-based SAF can reduce greenhouse gas intensity by 80–90% compared with conventional jet fuel, with gasification being the primary technical challenge. Incorporating green hydrogen further enhances mitigation, reducing emissions by up to 50% and enabling a reduction of over 170 kg of  $ CO_{2} $  per tonne of processed MSW. Globally, MSW-based SAF production could exceed 50 Mt  $ yr^{-1} $  (62.5 billion litres), offering a 16% reduction in aviation greenhouse gas emissions. In Europe, the estimated 5.4 Mt  $ yr^{-1} $  SAF capacity from this study exceeds the European Union blending mandate and complies with its restriction to non-food and feed feedstocks. Economic analysis indicates that using SAF to meet Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) targets can lead to substantial cost savings, particularly when subsidies are available.

Aviation is considered one of the most challenging sectors to achieve carbon neutrality, contributing 2.5% to global carbon emissions $ ^{1} $ . Due to the large energy required for aircraft to overcome gravity and air resistance, electrification and hydrogen-based alternatives face major challenges related to their low energy densities, particularly for long-haul flights $ ^{2} $ . Unlike light-duty road transportation, which is rapidly transitioning to electrification, there is no clear 'silver bullet' solution to achieve carbon-neutral aviation and a combination of strategies and innovations will be necessary $ ^{3} $ . Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) is regarded as a technically feasible solution as it can be directly blended into existing aviation fuel systems and burned by jet engines without extensive modifications $ ^{4} $ . It has been estimated that SAF could contribute to a more than 65% reduction in sector carbon emissions by 2050 (ref. 4). However, the current contribution from SAF is less than 1% of total fuel demand, limited primarily by high cost, low production capacity and limited operational experience $ ^{5} $ . Several countries



and regions have introduced incentive, or mandate-based, policies to promote the development and use of SAF $ ^{6} $ . For example, the USA has set an ambitious target to achieve an SAF production of 3 and 35 billion gallons (11.4 and 132.5 billion litres) per year by 2030 and 2050, respectively $ ^{7} $ , using a variety of incentives $ ^{8-10} $ . In Europe, the RefuelEU policy has mandated that all aircraft departing from European Union (EU) airports should use a minimum share of SAF in their fuel $ ^{11} $ . The required blend ratio will start at 2% in 2025 and rise to 70% by 2050. The Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) of the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) aims to encourage SAF usage through carbon pricing of fossil fuels $ ^{6} $ . Airlines can either offset the emissions from using jet fuel or use SAF to meet their obligations.

There are several technologically viable pathways for production of SAF using various feedstocks. These are conventionally referred to as the hydrotreated esters and fatty acids (HEFA), gasification–Fischer–Tropsch (FT), alcohol-to-jet (ATJ) and power-to-liquid (PTL) pathways $ ^{12} $ . Notably, these pathways have roots in the biofuel industry, which has evolved over decades, primarily for road transportation. However, the limitations of biofuels, currently produced primarily from food- and (animal) feed-based feedstocks, pose important challenges with respect to availability and carbon footprints $ ^{13} $ . Specifically, while HEFA, which mainly produces fuel from vegetable oil or waste oil, is currently the only commercially available pathway for SAF, it has been restricted or capped in many countries. This is due to the limited carbon mitigation effects caused by indirect land use and competing usage of its major feedstocks $ ^{11,14} $ . The ATJ and FT pathways are expected to be promising options in the coming decades $ ^{15} $ . The feedstocks for ATJ include ethanol and other alcohols, which are primarily produced from corn in the USA and sugarcane in Brazil $ ^{16} $ . These existing biorefineries will play crucial roles in the early commercialization of SAF, especially when combined with low-carbon technologies such as carbon capture to reduce emissions. The FT pathway is adaptable to a variety of carbon sources with gasification, such as agriculture and forest residue, energy crops, municipal solid waste (MSW) or even captured  $ CO_{2} $ . With the rapid development of green hydrogen produced from water electrolysis using renewable power, FT can be used to synthesize diverse carbon sources with green hydrogen to support the PTL pathway, which would mitigate concerns about feedstock availability $ ^{17,18} $ . The main concern with emerging ATJ and FT pathways is their high cost. Gasification–FT requires notable capital investment, while ATJ is highly sensitive to feedstock costs, as the value of the alcohol intermediate limits its cost-effectiveness for conversion to jet fuel $ ^{18} $ .

Rather than relying on food- and feed-based feedstocks, recent research has identified waste-based biomass as a more sustainable alternative $ ^{19} $ . One underresearched feedstock is MSW (commonly known as trash or garbage, consisting of everyday items used and then thrown away, including both biogenic materials such as food waste and paper and non-biogenic sources such as metal, plastic and rubber) $ ^{20} $ . With increasing MSW volumes and limited landfill space, incineration poses pollution risks and entails high emission control costs $ ^{21} $ . Converting MSW to liquid fuel is thus an attractive alternative $ ^{22} $ . Already routinely collected, comparatively abundant and facing fewer challenges of seasonal variation posed by other waste-based feedstocks such as crop residues, MSW presents a promising option for SAF feedstock. Most current efforts to convert MSW into SAF rely on the gasification–FT pathway $ ^{23} $ . However, compared with other feedstocks, MSW's heterogeneity in composition, moisture and size makes gasification particularly challenging $ ^{21,22,24,25} $ . Thus, most existing gasification facilities utilize fossil fuels or biomass, with limited experience exploring MSW $ ^{13,14,24,25} $ . For gasification–FT-based biorefineries, carbon conversion, gasification efficiency and syngas yield are critical to carbon emissions and economic viability, posing added challenges for MSW-based SAF (MSW-SAF) $ ^{24,26} $ . Specifically, MSW gasification requires preprocessing into relatively homogeneous, low-moisture refuse-derived fuel (RDF), alongside managing tar, impurities and particulates in syngas $ ^{25} $ . These requirements increase system complexity, energy consumption and costs. Previous studies have primarily relied on theoretical models based on borrowed data, forest or agricultural waste gasification, or diesel production data $ ^{26-31} $ . Therefore, it is important to involve real industrial-level data to accurately understand the key factors affecting greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, yield and the overall mitigation potential for MSW-SAF.



Here, the study proposes an application to synchronize the goals of zero-waste cities and carbon-neutral aviation by transforming the biogenic content of MSW into valuable low-carbon fuels. This study aims to assess the key challenges and decarbonization benefits of MSW-SAF to power sustainable aviation. By utilizing real-world operational data for key processes based on gasification–FT technology, the study identifies emission-intensive processes, quantifies the GHG footprint, analyses system sensitivities and explores potential options to enhance decarbonization benefits. Next, the analysis encompasses extensive data on MSW production and composition across various countries and cities, assessing its viability as a fuel source for aviation. Furthermore, the study examines the long-term economic and environmental impacts of MSW-SAF development by comparing it with the expected carbon offset costs of international flights (that is, CORSIA) $ ^{6} $ . This study highlights a pathway for transforming urban waste into sustainable aviation solutions, serving as a blueprint for future environmental and economic synergies.

## Assessment of life cycle GHG emissions of MSW-SAF

The model of life cycle GHG emissions developed in this study accounts for MSW collection and transportation, SAF production, transportation and combustion (Supplementary Fig. 1). The SAF production process is mainly composed of MSW pretreatment (homogenization, crushing, sorting, drying and pelletization), followed by gasification, the water–gas shift reaction, the Rectisol process (a trade name for acid gas separation), FT synthesis, and upgrading involving hydrocracking and hydrotreating. By utilizing real-world operational data from an MSW gasification plant and a FT technology plant (see details in the Methods), this study estimates a life cycle GHG emission intensity of 14.1 grams of  $ CO_{2} $  equivalent ( $ CO_{2e} $ ) per megajoule using 100% biogenic content of MSW (Fig. 1). This represents an 84% reduction with respect to that of conventional jet fuel. This study further identifies emission-intensive stages that are primarily influenced by the processing technology $ ^{32} $ . The most carbon-intensive processes of FT-based MSW-SAF include pretreatment, gasification and the Rectisol process. In particular, the Rectisol stage contributes 77% of the GHG emissions of the full process. This is largely due to the release of  $ CO_{2} $  generated in the water–gas shift reaction between CO and  $ H_{2}O $ , which is not captured or utilized. Pretreatment is another major contributor to emissions, primarily because of the high moisture content of raw MSW. To reduce moisture to below 20%, thermal drying is commonly applied, resulting in substantial energy use and associated carbon emissions.

The limited commercial deployment of MSW-derived SAF introduces considerable uncertainty in life cycle emissions estimates $ ^{[13,14,24,25,29]} $  (see Supplementary Table 1 for more biorefineries). As shown in Fig. 1c, the composition of MSW and the system's energy mix are major drivers of this variability, although both can be adjusted through process design. By contrast, a more intrinsic source of uncertainty stems from technical limitations in the gasification process, primarily due to the heterogeneous and complex nature of MSW, which presents substantial challenges for both gasification and pretreatment $ ^{[25,29]} $ . This study adopts a baseline scenario with a syngas effective composition  $ (H_{2} + CO) $  of 70%, and a yield of  $ 0.9 \, Nm^{3} \, kg^{-1} $  of RDF, based on industrial level data (Supplementary Table 2). However, evidence from various gasification facilities suggests that feedstock characteristics and gasifier technologies have substantial influence.

To evaluate sensitivity under broader conditions, we incorporate data from other biomass gasification processes $ ^{33-39} $  (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2). Our analysis shows that a reduction in syngas effective composition to 50% leads to a 60% increase in life cycle GHG emissions (22.5 gCO $ _{2} $ e MJ $ ^{-1} $ ) due to additional energy consumption required to maintain output. Simultaneously, the yield of fuel decreases by 29%, thereby constraining the pathway's overall mitigation potential. In addition, variations in syngas yield within the range of 0.8–1.1 Nm $ ^{3} $  kg $ ^{-1} $  of RDF lead to emissions changes from +19% to –27%. These underscore the critical influence of gasification performance.

Beyond technical parameters, emissions are also shaped by energy and material inputs across the production system, with electricity as a major contributor. Using renewable electricity can reduce life cycle emissions by up to 75%. However, MSW management practices contribute the most variability and remain difficult to standardize. Among these, the biogenic carbon content of the feedstock is particularly important. Even after sorting and drying, MSW suitable for gasification typically contains fossil-based plastics, resulting in an average biogenic content of 78% (ref. 40) (Supplementary Table 4). Although plastics contribute to higher heating values and improved gasification reactivity, they can increase life cycle emissions by up to 99%. Enhancing the biogenic share in the feedstock is therefore an effective strategy for emission reduction. In addition to feedstock composition, high moisture content and lack of segregation in mixed MSW complicate pretreatment $ ^{21} $ , particularly increasing energy use for heating, which contributes an average of 20% to life cycle emissions in MSW-SAF processing $ ^{21} $ . This challenge is more pronounced in developing countries, where food waste shares are higher (81%) than in developed countries (74%) $ ^{41} $ . Adopting low-energy drying methods such as bio-drying can reduce emissions at this stage. Mechanical operations such as separation and shredding also contribute substantially due to electricity use. These burdens can be mitigated through improved upstream waste segregation, which reduces the need for intensive pretreatment and lowers life cycle emissions.



## Enhancing decarbonization benefits and carbon efficiency

The SAF industry faces increasing constraints on feedstock availability, making the carbon efficiency of feedstock-to-fuel conversion a critical factor in its overall mitigation potential. Although MSW-SAF offers substantial life cycle GHG reduction benefits, its overall carbon conversion efficiency remains limited at only 33% (Supplementary Fig. 3), primarily due to the mismatch between the composition of raw syngas from MSW gasification and the ideal ratios needed for FT synthesis. Specifically, the FT synthesis process requires a  $ CO:H_{2} $  ratio of approximately 1:2, whereas the ratio in the raw gas from MSW is 1:1 (Supplementary Table 5). Consequently, a substantial amount of carbon (in the form of CO) is wasted in the water–gas shift reaction to generate  $ H_{2} $ . Two alternative approaches are therefore proposed to enhance carbon efficiency: capturing the  $ CO_{2} $  released during the Rectisol process or directly introducing hydrogen instead of relying on the water–gas shift reaction (Fig. 2).

In the first option, the released gas offers a substantial potential for  $ CO_{2} $  capture given its high concentration (97%). While capturing and directly storing  $ CO_{2} $  can achieve a net carbon intensity of  $ -59~g~CO_{2}e~MJ^{-1} $ , this would be a waste of a scarce sustainable biomass carbon source from the perspective of producing fuels. An alternative involves using the captured  $ CO_{2} $  for SAF production via the PTL technology pathway (MSW-PTL), which can achieve carbon conversion efficiency greater than 60%. This study examines the potential for integrating urban MSW management with local jet fuel supply (Fig. 3a,c). Results show that the highest MSW-SAF potential is concentrated in large, densely populated cities. Megacities such as Beijing, Shanghai, Cairo, New York, São Paulo and London could each produce over 100 kt yr $ ^{-1} $ . However, these cities also have high jet fuel demand, resulting in lower SAF supply shares. By contrast, cities in low-income countries tend to have higher SAF supply shares, averaging over 30%, compared with 12% in high-income and 15–19% in middle-income cities (Supplementary Fig. 4). Differences in MSW-SAF potential across cities are closely linked to air travel intensity (Supplementary Fig. 5). In cities where residents take more than two flights per year, SAF supply meets less than 10% of demand on average. Conversely, cities with one flight per capita can meet over half of their jet fuel needs through MSW conversion. Data from the International Air Transport Association and major airlines show that there are numerous cities where the average person takes about one flight annually $ ^{45} $ , indicating considerable potential for localized MSW-to-SAF strategies in cities with moderate aviation demand and sufficient waste availability.

## Carbon abatement costs through CORSIA and SAF implementation

A primary concern of industry stakeholders is the cost-effectiveness of MSW-SAF compared with jet fuel and other alternatives. In this study, production costs are projected to decline from US1,114–2,125 per tonne (US0.89–1.70 per litre) in the pioneer phase to US750–1,352 per tonne (US0.60–1.08 per litre) as facilities scale (Supplementary Fig. 6), approaching current SAF market prices (higher than fossil jet fuel) $ ^{46} $ . These estimates are based on existing MSW gasification and FT plants with scale adjustments and are comparable to but lower than those reported in previous studies (Supplementary Table 7). The assessment indicates that economic viability of MSW-SAF depends heavily on technology maturity and system performance. Cost differences between the conservative (S1) and baseline (S2) scenarios range from 43% to 70%. Among these, MSW management practices play a critical role, as gate or tipping fees can offset sorting and processing expenses. While this study assumes zero feedstock cost, a US10 per tonne charge could raise SAF production costs by US140. Pretreatment adds roughly US54 per tonne in operating expenses and requires capital investment exceeding US90 million (2015 US dollars). Gasification and FT synthesis are highly capital intensive, with capital costs accounting for -70% of total production costs and varying by up to 50% between pioneer and nth-of-a-kind plants. Operating expenditures, primarily associated with catalyst lifetime and efficiency losses during conversion, can contribute up to 25% of total production costs (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To assess the economic viability of MSW-SAF, this study compares its cost with that of meeting emissions obligations under CORSIA—the first global sectoral carbon reduction scheme, which becomes mandatory in 2027. Airlines can comply by purchasing carbon offsets or using eligible SAF $ ^{14,47} $ . China and the USA are selected for analysis owing to their leading aviation emissions and large MSW-SAF potential (Fig. 4). The evaluation considers MSW-SAF costs under conservative (S1) and baseline (S2) scenarios, assuming a linear cost trajectory from the pioneer phase in 2020 to the mature phase in 2030. It also accounts for US subsidy programmes, including the Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) and California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS), as well as carbon prices under CORSIA. The analysis shows that, between 2027 and 2035, cumulative offset costs under CORSIA in both countries are projected to reach US8 to US10 billion. Under the baseline (S2) scenario, where SAF production costs decline and offset prices rise, SAF adoption offers long-term cost advantages. Specifically, the median abatement cost of SAF is projected to decline from around US150 per tonne of  $ CO_{2} $  in 2025 to below US50 by 2030, while carbon offset prices are expected to exceed US50 by then. This suggests 2030 as the break-even point when SAF becomes cost-comparable with offsets. Over the full implementation period, China could achieve cumulative savings exceeding US2 billion by using MSW-SAF, while the USA could save around US1 billion (Fig. 4a,c). Under a high-offset-price scenario, benefits could surpass US10 billion, and with US federal subsidies, savings may reach US18 billion (Fig. 4e). China's greater cost advantage is largely attributed to its abundant MSW resources. To meet CORSIA obligations with SAF by 2035, China would need to utilize only 70% of its available MSW, while the USA could meet just 75% of its SAF requirement from domestic MSW.



Under the conservative (S1) scenario, where technological progress is limited and system efficiency remains low, the MSW-to-SAF conversion potential is substantially reduced. In this case, SAF supply would meet only about 70% of offset requirements in China and roughly 40% in the USA, making it insufficient for full compliance. Moreover, the marginal abatement cost of SAF exceeds projected CORSIA offset prices, reducing its economic attractiveness and limiting voluntary adoption by airlines (Fig. 4b,d). Nonetheless, even under these less favourable conditions, the US subsidy scenario remains cost-effective: cumulative savings could reach US$2.1 billion, or over US$10 billion under high-offset-price conditions (Fig. 4f). These findings underscore the importance of targeted subsidies in accelerating SAF deployment. Where technological progress is slow and cost reductions are limited, financial incentives are essential to drive adoption and enable scale. In turn, large-scale deployment is critical for driving innovation and achieving long-term cost competitiveness.

## Discussion

MSW management remains a major challenge for modern economies. As land becomes scarcer and environmental concerns increase, waste disposal has shifted from landfilling to waste-to-energy solutions $ ^{21,29} $ . Converting MSW into liquid fuel offers a promising sustainable solution for global decarbonization efforts $ ^{29} $ . The study introduces industrial-level data to demonstrate that MSW is a promising feedstock for producing SAF for the hard-to-abate aviation sector. Through pretreatment, gasification and FT synthesis, MSW-derived SAF can achieve an 80–90% reduction in lifecycle GHG emissions. Converting all global MSW to SAF could reduce global aviation GHG emissions by 16% in 2019. Notably, MSW is a near-zero-cost feedstock, making MSW-SAF a potentially cost-effective option. This approach offers both environmental and economic benefits, supporting the aviation sector's transition to a low-carbon future.

The lack of large-scale commercial experience for MSW-derived SAF creates considerable uncertainty in life cycle emissions and cost estimates $ ^{[13,24,25,29]} $  (see Supplementary Table 3 for more biorefineries). Modelling results show that, when pretreatment and gasification operate at suboptimal efficiency, life cycle GHG emissions from MSW-SAF can nearly double and fuel yield may decline by up to 50%, reducing the overall mitigation potential by approximately 60%. In such cases, MSW-SAF becomes more costly than meeting CORSIA obligations through carbon offsets, diminishing its competitiveness and adoption potential. These findings underscore the crucial role of gasification in determining the technical and economic viability of MSW to SAF. More broadly, gasification is advancing rapidly, driven by its high carbon conversion efficiency and compatibility with diverse feedstocks, and is receiving increasing attention across sectors. With ongoing improvements in low-energy drying and upstream energy decarbonization, emission intensity could be further reduced by up to 60% compared with the current case, increasing the mitigation potential to more than 75%. These results reinforce the long-term promise of the MSW-to-SAF pathway as part of a diversified portfolio of SAF.

## Methods

## Life cycle GHG emissions

The production processes are derived from real-world operational data, with data on MSW preprocessing sourced from an MSW gasification plant $ ^{40} $  and those for the SAF production process from a commercialized FT plant $ ^{52} $  (Supplementary Tables 2 and 9). The distribution of final products follows the aviation kerosene scheme of the FT plant, using the energy allocation method from the greenhouse gases, regulated emissions, and energy use in technologies (GREET) model $ ^{53} $  (Supplementary Table 10). The analysis of GHG emissions is formulated in terms of grams of  $ CO_{2}e $  per megajoule. The  $ CO_{2}e $  effects of non- $ CO_{2} $  species  $ CH_{4} $  and  $ N_{2}O $  were estimated using their 100-year global warming potential (25 and 298) in line with United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change guidelines $ ^{54} $ . Contrails are not included in this study owing to their large uncertainty, despite their substantial contribution to warming $ ^{55} $ . As a baseline, the GHG emission intensity of conventional jet fuel is taken as 89 g  $ CO_{2}e $   $ MJ^{-1} $ , in line with the estimation in CORSIA. The material and energy inputs and outputs across all stages of the MSW-SAF pathway can be found in Supplementary Table 11. To produce 1 MJ of SAF, approximately 0.18 kg of MSW (processed) will be required. Along with MSW, the input requires oxygen for gasification, steam (utilized for heat) and electricity to support system operations, while the outputs include jet fuel, heat generated during the reaction and other associated by-products. The GHG emission intensities of the energy and materials used in the model are based on localized industrial operations in China $ ^{56} $  (Supplementary Tables 4 and 12). The avoided  $ CH_{4} $  emissions and credits for additional recycled plastic and metal are based on the CORSIA default values $ ^{57} $ .

## Mitigation technologies

Various modifications and adjustments have been implemented for advanced technology pathways (MSW- $ H_{2} $  and MSW-PTL). For the MSW- $ H_{2} $  pathway, the hydrogen volume is estimated to maintain the same total molar amount of  $ CO + H_{2} $  as in the raw synthesis gas (Supplementary Table 5). Renewable hydrogen is produced through electrolysis with renewable electricity, while the by-product oxygen can be utilized in the gasification process, resulting in negative GHG emissions as large as  $ -1.36  kgCO_{2} kg^{-1} H_{2} $ . In the MSW-PTL pathway, approximately 96.7% of the released  $ CO_{2} $  during the Rectisol process is captured with  $ 0.042  t CO_{2} t^{-1} $  additional energy input and  $ 0.033  t CO_{2} t^{-1} $  loss. The introduction of renewable hydrogen ( $ 16.6  g H_{2} MJ^{-1} SAF $ ) allows for production of synthetic gas through RWGS, enabling the continued production of SAF through FT synthesis $ ^{17} $ . The energy-intensive nature of the RWGS process is recognized, necessitating consumption of  $ 0.05  kWh MJ^{-1} $  electricity and  $ 0.19  MJ MJ^{-1} $  steam.

## MSW-SAF potential

To assess the feasibility of SAF production from MSW, the methodology initiates an analysis of MSW availability and aviation fuel demand on both national and city scales. This approach involves a systematic examination of local MSW generation patterns and aviation fuel consumption rates, establishing a foundational understanding of the potential for SAF synthesis in specified areas. The national-level MSW data, including volume and composition, are derived from World Bank statistics $ ^{41} $ . The city-level analysis utilizes multiple data sources, including direct inputs from the World Bank for international cities and China's Statistical Yearbook for Chinese cities $ ^{58,59} $ . Due to the limited availability of data for cities of Europe and the USA, the study estimated this product from population figures for major European and US cities together with their national per capita MSW outputs $ ^{60-63} $ . To determine the SAF conversion potential, the study investigated the dry biogenic content of MSW according to country-specific waste compositions and applied conversion rates through the MSW-SAF process. Key assumptions regarding MSW-to-SAF conversion potential and life cycle emission factors under different scenarios (S1: conservative; S2: current practice; S3: ambitious with renewable hydrogen, MSW-H $ _{2} $ ) are detailed in Supplementary Table 6. Our analysis of MSW-SAF potential is based on data from the most recent available year (mostly 2019), but it is important to note that factors such as population growth, changes in per capita MSW generation and recycling rates could influence long-term SAF potential (Supplementary Fig. 8). To estimate fuel consumption, the analysis used a model derived from actual flight trajectories, encompassing data from more than 10,000 flights per day in 2019 (refs. 64,65). The study organized this extensive dataset according to the origin of each flight, categorizing it in terms of both the originating country and city. For per capita air travel assessments, the study used flight numbers and seat availability data from the OAG global flight dataset $ ^{66} $ , with the seat occupancy factor of 0.82 reported by the International Air Transport Association $ ^{67} $ . Data analysis was performed using Matlab R2023b and Microsoft Excel Version 2506.



## Cost of abatement

The primary expenses associated with global aviation carbon emission reductions are derived from CORSIA and the adoption of SAF. Carbon offset calculations within CORSIA are determined by state pairs, requiring offsets only for flights between participating departure and arrival states or countries. The scheme comprises three phases: a voluntary pilot phase (2021–2023), a voluntary phase I (2024–2026) and a mandatory phase II (2027–2035) for all member states $ ^{6} $  except those with revenue tonne kilometres (representing the volume of air transport activity) ranking below 10% and less than 0.5% of total revenue tonne kilometres in 2018 (ref. 68). This research focused on the USA and China. Each airline's offsetting requirements for 2027–2035 are determined on the basis of the growth of the sector and their own emissions. The analysis derived baseline emissions for 2019 from ICAO statistics $ ^{43} $ , focusing on state pairs within the scope of the CORSIA scheme. For future projections, the study utilized global forecasts of international aviation carbon emissions from ICAO $ ^{69} $ . Specifically, for the international aviation emissions of the two countries, the estimates are based on the demand growth in connected international markets as reported by Boeing $ ^{70} $ , and carbon efficiency improvements from the ICAO's Long-Term Aspirational Goal framework $ ^{71,72} $  (Supplementary Table 13). The cost of offsetting is influenced by the trading price of CORSIA-Eligible Units, which presently averages below US10 per metric ton of CO $ _{2} $  (ref. 47). Projected changes in carbon offset prices from 2027 to 2035 draw on forecasts provided by multiple institutions $ ^{73-76} $  (Supplementary Table 14) and assume a linear growth trajectory. The carbon offset price in 2030 is projected at US50 per tonne, with a range of US6.5 to US100. To evaluate the economic implications of CORSIA implementation, we examined uncertainties in both aviation market growth and carbon offset prices. Sensitivity analysis showed that uncertainty in carbon offset prices is the dominant driver, with an impact over ten times greater than that of market size. Therefore, the analysis focused primarily on the influence of carbon offset price fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 9). Utilizing SAF can offset emissions requirements, with the amount of the reduction determined by the quantity of SAF used and the life cycle emissions reduction compared with conventional jet fuel. The associated costs of emission reductions are derived from the differences in cost between SAF and jet fuel, with or without subsidies. The emissions reduction percentage for SAF follows the default values set by CORSIA $ ^{77} $ . In some of the

scenarios, the potential for MSW-SAF is insufficient to meet all offset demands. Therefore, costs are calculated on the basis of maximizing the use of MSW-SAF, with remaining requirements covered by costs for conventional offsets.

The cost analysis of SAF draws on real data from MSW gasification and FT facilities, with adjustments for scale effects (Supplementary Tables 15–17). Estimated SAF costs range from US1,114 to US2,125 per tonne in the pioneer phase and from US750 to US1,352 per tonne in the mature (nth) phase (Supplementary Fig. 6). The cost structure of the gasification–FT process is sensitive particularly to capital costs, with equipment investments estimated at US695 million (2015 values), roughly twice the operating expense (Supplementary Tables 7 and 16). Regarding operating expense, the cobalt-based catalyst lifetime is assumed at 3 years $ ^{23} $ , contributing to around 15% of the operational expenditure (Supplementary Table 16). To enhance the global applicability of the SAF cost estimates, this study conducted a comprehensive review of waste-based feedstocks and integrated literature-based values with model-derived results (Supplementary Table 7). We used the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles to represent low, medium and high cost assumptions, respectively, for both pioneer and mature plants. The cost trajectory is assumed to follow a linear trend from the pioneer phase in 2020 to the mature phase in 2030, with the lower cost boundary set at the current jet fuel price of US750 per tonne $ ^{78} $ . Notably, the cost analysis focuses on the conservative (S1) and baseline (S2) scenarios (Supplementary Fig. 10), as the marginal abatement costs of the MSW- $ H_{2} $  and MSW-PTL pathways remain substantially higher (Supplementary Fig. 11). In terms of policy support, several SAF-related incentives are currently in place in the USA. These include the RFS, the LCFS and a number of federal and state-level tax credit programmes $ ^{8-10,49} $ . While tax credits can substantially improve SAF economics, their continuation beyond 2027 remains uncertain. To maintain a conservative and consistent baseline, this study includes only the RFS and LCFS in the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12). The RFS subsidy is determined by the generation of 1.6 renewable identification numbers (RIN) credits per gallon of SAF and the corresponding RIN price (ranging from US0.53 to US1.59 per RIN), while the LCFS subsidy is based on the amount of carbon emissions reduced per gallon and the applicable LCFS credit price (ranging from US90.4 to US190.0 per tonne of  $ CO_{2} $ ) $ ^{8,9} $ . Because both RIN and LCFS prices vary over time, this study uses average values from 2019 to 2024.
