Electrochemical  $ CO_{2} $  reduction can generate multi-carbon  $ (C_{2+}) $  products via a  $ CO_{2} $ -to-CO cascade followed by CO reduction (COR). However, COR energy efficiency remains below 40% due to sluggish ion transport within charge-selective membranes. Here we introduce an uncharged porous separator that enables facile transport of both ion types, reducing ohmic resistance and superconcentrating cations at the catalyst surface—lowering COR voltage by 150 mV at 200 mA  $ cm^{-2} $ . In previous electrolyser designs, porous separators were limited by cathode-to-anode  $ H_{2} $  crossover; the low diffusivity of  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  and CO in water allows a separator three times thinner and 1.6 times more porous, markedly reducing overpotential. Operating at elevated temperatures with a nickel–iron-based anode further lowers voltage by 330 mV, leading to a full-cell voltage of 1.95 V at 200 mA  $ cm^{-2} $  and an energy efficiency of 51% to  $ C_{2+} $  products sustained over 250 h. The system also achieves a CO single-pass conversion of 97% and a  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  concentration of 87 wt% in the product gas stream.

Electrochemical  $ CO_{2} $  reduction ( $ CO_{2}R $ ), when powered by renewable energy, offers a means to transform waste  $ CO_{2} $  into valuable multi-carbon ( $ C_{2+} $ ) chemicals and fuels $ ^{1,2} $ . Although the field has advanced rapidly, high energy input presents a barrier to wider application $ ^{3,4} $ .  $ CO_{2}R $  in alkaline media enables the generation of  $ C_{2+} $  products $ ^{5-8} $ ; however, under alkaline conditions, the reactant  $ CO_{2} $  converts to carbonate, and regenerating this to  $ CO_{2} $  is costly $ ^{9,10} $ . Performing  $ CO_{2}R $  in acidic electrolytes, and employing bipolar membranes to regenerate  $ CO_{2} $ , addresses the  $ CO_{2} $  loss problem, but the energy efficiency (EE) remains below 20% (Fig. 1a) $ ^{9,11,12} $ .

Achieving both carbon and energy efficiency is possible through cascade  $ CO_{2}R $ , starting with  $ CO_{2} $ -to-CO conversion followed by CO reduction (COR). The first  $ CO_{2} $ -to-CO step can be performed in a high-temperature solid-oxide electrolyser with an EE that exceeds 80% $ ^{13,14} $ . Thus, realizing the full promise of the cascade approach will require efficiency gains in the COR step.



Operating COR in alkaline conditions benefits from improved kinetics and minimal Nernstian pH losses $ ^{8,15,16} $ . COR is typically performed in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) in which the cathode and anode are separated by an anion exchange membrane (AEM, Fig. 1b). The AEM provides a physical separation and controls ion, liquid and gas transport between electrodes. This has led to EEs that have reached a peak value of 40% so far towards  $ C_{2+} $  products (Fig. 1a) $ ^{14,17-20} $ . Even with such progress in the field, the cost of (desirably low-carbon-intensity) electrolyser electricity is forecast to dominate cost (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Notes 2 and 3).

This process has a kinetic limit owing to the energy required to break the electrostatic interactions between the hydroxide ions at each positively charged site, as well as the energy required for continuous hydration/dehydration $ ^{21,22} $ . As a result, present-day AEMs exhibit low conductivity, a challenge shared with AEM fuel cells and electrolysers $ ^{23,24} $ .

In this work, we introduce a porous hydrophilic separator as an alternative to charge-selective membranes in COR electrolysers. This separator enables efficient ion transport while blocking gas crossover and creates a local cation superconcentration at the cathode, reducing cell voltage and improving EE. Compared with AEM-based systems, the porous-separator approach lowers full-cell voltage by 150 mV at 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ . We further integrate a nickel–iron anode and operate at elevated temperature, achieving a full-cell voltage of 1.95 V and an EE of 51% toward C $ _{2+} $  products that was sustained over 250 h.

## Limitations of AEM and membrane-free systems

To baseline AEM system performance, we performed COR using a Cu cathode (scanning electron microscopy image in Supplementary Fig. 3) and an iridium oxide (IrOx) anode separated by an AEM. When we used a highly alkaline anolyte, the  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  and  $ C_{2+} $  Faradaic efficiency (FE) increased with applied current density, reaching 35% and 86% at current densities over 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4). We measured a full-cell voltage of 2.43 V at 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , corresponding to an EE of 35% for  $ C_{2+} $  products and 16% for  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  (Supplementary Fig. 5). This result is typical of the field $ ^{[14,17-20]} $ .

However, we observed substantial cathode gas crossover to the anode compartment, losing over 20% of the  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  to the anode (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Additionally, we detected  $ O_{2} $  generated by the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER, corresponding to ~10% of the  $ O_{2} $  produced) in the cathodic gas outlet. It is expected that gas bubbles generated at the electrode surface rise through the central electrolyte via buoyancy-driven convection and, in the absence of a membrane, mix with the cathode products. Increasing the interelectrode thickness reduced gas crossover but substantially raised cell voltage (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 9). This interelectrode thickness (~0.3 mm, over five times the thickness of the AEM) was necessary to prevent a short circuit.

The membrane-free system did have low hydrogen-evolution reaction (FE < 5%) and good selectivity towards  $ C_{2+} $  products ( $ FE_{C_{2+}} > 90\% $ , Supplementary Fig. 10). AEMs, while not perfectly selective, serve to block alkali cation migration to the cathode catalyst. In contrast, in the membrane-free system, bulk electrolyte readily permits the passage of solvated cations, and these can build up at the interface between the hydrophobic support and the hydrophilic ionomer-coated catalyst layer, forming a hydrophobic:hydrophilic abrupt junction. We modelled the concentration of solvated cations at the junction, finding it to reach 4.5 M for the membrane-free approach, double that of the AEM case (Fig. 1d). The establishment of a cation superconcentration near the cathode may contribute to high  $ C_{2+} $  productivity $ ^{15,26,27} $ .

We noted the chief reason why AWE employs separators in this regime of thickness and porosity: in the hydrogen-evolution reaction, dissolved  $ H_{2} $  diffuses rapidly through water-filled pores, and the field focuses thus on reducing water permeability (achieved through the use of small pores) and on increasing thickness to slow  $ H_{2} $  diffusion.

In contrast, in COR to  $ C_{2}H_{4} $ , both feedstock and product diffuse much more slowly in water. Even when using a thin and highly porous separator, we found that gas product crossover was similar to that in AEM COR (Fig. 1f), with each approach providing  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  below 0.1 vol.% on the anode. We investigated how separator thickness, material composition and pore size affect COR performance (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Figs. 12–14). These results reveal clear trends: cell voltage and  $ C_{2+} $  FE improved with thinner and more porous materials.

To establish a key descriptor for the separator in CO-to- $ C_{2+} $  electrolysis, we correlated performance trends with measured water permeability (Fig. 2a–c). Higher permeability shortened hydrated ion transport pathways and enabled greater cation concentration at the catalyst, contributing to improved selectivity and reduced cell voltage. Specifically, a separator with -50–100- $ \mu $ m thickness, pore sizes of >0.45  $ \mu $ m and porosity of >80% (see scanning electron microscopy images in Supplementary Fig. 15) resulted in the lowest full-cell voltages and highest  $ C_{2+} $  partial current densities (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 14). However, performance plateaued beyond a threshold water permeability, indicating that ion migration toward the cathode was no longer the limiting factor.



With the thin and highly porous separator and the abrupt hydrophobic:hydrophilic junction, the system achieved a  $ C_{2+} $  FE of 95% and  $ H_{2} $  FE below 5% at current densities beyond 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 16). In contrast with the large gap in the membrane-free cell, and also with the 200–300- $ \mu $ m gap of AWE separators, the separator enabled an  $ \sim $ 60- $ \mu $ m separation between the electrodes. At 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , this led to a full-cell voltage of 2.28 V, a 150-mV reduction compared with the AEM case (Fig. 2e). This resulted in a C $ _{2} $ H $ _{4} $  EE of 19% and a C $ _{2+} $  EE of 41% (Fig. 2f). We investigated the origin of the reduced voltage by performing full-cell electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Supplementary Fig. 19) to measure series resistance,  $ R_{s} $ . We observed that  $ R_{s} $  decreased from 0.8 to 0.4  $ \Omega $  cm $ ^{2} $  when we switched to the separator. We further resolved all internal voltages and found that the separator reduced ohmic resistance by approximately 80 mV and lowered the cathode overpotential by about 60 mV (Fig. 3b). We attribute the latter improvement to cation superconcentration at the cathode, which enhances activity and reduces the kinetic barrier to  $ C_{2} $  product formation.

We further measured the electrochemical active surface area, looking for indications that a catalyst directly wetted by electrolyte through the hydrophilic domains could enhance the triple phase boundary. We measured double-layer capacitances via cyclic voltammetry scans in the non-Faradaic potential range (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig.20) and found the electrochemical active surface area at 8 cm $ ^{2} $  compared with 1 cm $ ^{2} $  for the AEM.



## Enhancing performance through anode catalyst and temperature optimization

We turned to the anode catalyst to further lower the full-cell voltage. Earth-abundant Ni-based catalysts have been reported to outperform Ir-based catalysts for alkaline OER $ ^{32-34} $ . We therefore replaced IrOx with a nickel–iron–boride anode (NiFe–B) (Supplementary Figs. 21–25). The product distribution from COR was unaltered (Supplementary Fig. 26). Operating at 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $ , the NiFe–B catalyst enabled a full-cell voltage of 2.15 V (Fig. 4a) and an increased C $ _{2} $ H $ _{4} $  EE of 20% and C $ _{2} $ . EE of 43% (Fig. 4b). A voltage distribution analysis showed that NiFe–B reduced the anodic overpotential by 100 mV (Supplementary Fig. 27), and the half-cell EIS further showed that the catalyst enhances charge transfer, providing accelerated OER kinetics under alkaline conditions (Supplementary Fig. 27).

The thermal stability of the separator allows for experiments at elevated temperatures—conditions that would accelerate chemical degradation in conventional AEMs $ ^{35,36} $ . We varied operating temperature by adding an external heat source (Fig. 4c–e). Elevated operating temperatures can increase EE by accelerating kinetics of both the cathodic COR and anodic OER and increasing the conductivity of the electrolyte $ ^{37} $ . At higher temperatures, we observed first a reduction in  $ H_{2} $  FE coinciding with a slight improvement in  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  FE and  $ C_{2+} $  FE to over 55% and 96%, respectively (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 28). The peak  $ C_{2}H_{4} $  FE and  $ C_{2+} $  FE shifted towards lower current densities with increased temperature, a finding we attribute to decreased CO solubility and more optimal CO coverage for  $ C_{2+} $  generation $ ^{38-40} $ . Ultimately, we achieved a

full-cell voltage (Fig. 4c) of 1.95 V and a  $ C_{2+} $  EE of 51% at 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  by optimizing to 50 °C (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 5). We found that the elevated temperature primarily reduced the cathodic overpotential while also slightly lowering the ohmic resistance and anodic overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 29). Through optimization, we achieved a cumulative voltage reduction of ~500 mV at 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  compared with the baseline AEM COR case (Supplementary Fig. 30).

## Long-term stability and CO single-pass conversion

Achieving extended operating stability has been a challenge in COR systems. We conducted prolonged electrolysis galvanostatically at  $ 200 \, mA \, cm^{-2} $  and  $ 50 \, ^{\circ}C $  (Fig. 5a). The system remained stable for  $ 250 \, operating\ hours $ , with EE decaying by less than 10% by the end of the stability run (Supplementary Table 4). To further challenge the stability of the separator material, we reused the separator after the stability run with new electrodes. The performance was comparable to that with a fresh one, indicating that even after long run times the separator is not limiting (Supplementary Fig. 31).

We then sought to increase CO single-pass conversion efficiency (SPCE) by tuning the operating conditions of the electrolyser (Fig. 5b). High CO SPCE increases product concentration at the outlet, decreasing the downstream separation energy $ ^{41,42} $ . At 200 mA cm $ ^{-2} $  at a flow rate of 0.75 sccm cm $ ^{-2} $ , we achieved SPCE of 97% towards C $ _{2+} $  products (Fig. 5b), with the C $ _{2} $ H $ _{4} $  FE and C $ _{2+} $  FE maintained at 56% and 90%. The result is a C $ _{2} $ H $ _{4} $  concentration at the outlet of 87 wt% (or 54 vol.%, Fig. 5c). The porous separator and resulting hydrophobic:hydrophilic abrupt junction contribute to these improvements in FE, EE, SPCE and stability towards C $ _{2+} $  products (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 5).

## Conclusion

We find that a porous hydrophilic domain blocks gas crossover, allows rapid ion conduction and provides a cation superconcentration that accelerates COR. This approach increases EE, prolongs operating stability and supports high SPCE. This work highlights the potential for microporous components to improve upon charge-selective membranes in electrolysers. Beyond the COR electrolyser, other systems limited by ion transport could benefit, particularly those with anode and cathode chambers that differ in phase (for example, a gas-fed cathode and liquid-fed anode as here, or the reverse). The robustness of the separator material offers further opportunities to operate in harsh conditions, such as high temperatures, strongly acidic or alkaline electrolytes, or the presence of produced alcohols that can degrade conventional membrane materials. The performance, simplicity and durability of this approach advance  $ CO_{2} $  and CO conversion toward industrial application.

## Methods

## Synthesis of the NiFe–B catalyst

The NiFe–B catalyst was synthesized using a method reported previously $ ^{[34]} $ . Briefly, 332.8 mg of  $ NiCl_{2}\cdot6H_{2}O $  (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 227.1 mg of anhydrous  $ FeCl_{3} $  ( $ \geq99.99\% $ , Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 2 ml of deionized water. In a separate vial, 2 ml of 5 M  $ NaBH_{4} $  ( $ \geq98\% $ , Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared in deionized water. All solutions were cooled in an ice bath for 10 min to prevent uncontrolled hydrolysis to avoid formation of precipitates. The Ni and Fe precursor solution was then added dropwise to the  $ NaBH_{4} $  solution. The resulting black powder was then washed and centrifuged with water and ethanol three times each before vacuum drying.

## Electrode preparation

Cathodes were prepared by spray-coating a Cu ink onto hydrophobic carbon paper. To prepare the ink, 75 mg of Cu nanoparticles (25-nm particle size, Sigma-Aldrich) and 250  $ \mu $ l of Nafion solution (5 wt% in mixture of lower aliphatic alcohols and water, contains 45% water, Sigma-Aldrich) were dispersed in 5 ml of methanol. The ink mixture was then sonicated for at least 1 h before being spray-coated on a 5 × 5 cm $ ^{2} $  hydrophobic carbon paper (Sigracet 39 BB, Fuel Cell Store). The cathode was then air-dried overnight before use. Before the test, the cathode was cut to 1 × 1 cm $ ^{2} $  for measurement.



For the stability test, homemade hydrophobic carbon paper was used instead. The homemade hydrophobic carbon paper was made by blade-casting a 0.5-mm layer of mixed PTFE and carbon nanoparticle ink over a  $ 15 \times 20 \, cm^2 $  carbon paper gas diffusion layer (AvCarb MGL 190 with 50% wet proof, Fuel Cell Earth). The carbon ink was prepared by mixing 0.4 g of carbon nanoparticles with 35 ml of isopropanol and four drops of Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich). The PTFE dispersion was prepared by mixing 4 g of PTFE preparation (60 wt% dispersion in  $ H_2O $ , Sigma-Aldrich) and 6 g of deionized water. Before the blade-casting, the carbon ink and the PTFE dispersion were each vortexed for 1 min, then mixed and stirred for an additional 40 s. After blade-casting, the resulting substrate was air-dried for 3 h before being placed in an oven for 3 h at 280 °C and 1 h at 340 °C. The resulting substrate was then used as the carbon paper for the stability test.

IrOx anodes were purchased from Magneto Special Anodes (Ir-mixed metal oxide (MMO)-coated titanium felt), cut to  $ 1 \times 1 \, cm^{2} $  and used directly. To prepare the NiFe-B anode, 40 mg of the synthesized NiFe-B powder and 133  $ \mu $ l of Nafion solution (5 wt%) were dispersed in 3 ml of methanol. The ink mixture was then sonicated for at least 1 h. The resulting dispersion was then spray-coated onto a  $ 2.5 \times 5 \, cm^{2} $  IrOx anode.

## Electrolyser configuration

All experiments were performed in a customized electrolyser (316L stainless-steel cathode plate and grade 2 titanium anode plate) with an active area of  $ 1 \, cm^{2} $  accessed with a serpentine channel. To prevent liquid leakage around the electrode edges into the flow channel, the cathode electrode was securely affixed to the flow plate using adhesive tape. The MEA experiments were performed with an AEM (Sustainment X37-50 Grade RT, Dioxide Materials), which was sandwiched between the cathode and the anode. Both the cathode and the anode were protected and surrounded by a 0.01-in-thick silicone gasket to provide leak proofing. For the systems with separators, the set-up remained the same except that the AEM was replaced by a hydrophilic separator placed between the cathode and the anode. Unless otherwise specified, 20 sccm of CO (high purity, 2.5 grade, Praxair or Linde) was fed into the cathode while the anode was circulated with an electrolyte at a rate of  $ 5 \, ml min^{-1} $ . Heating pads were used to heat the electrolyser for experiments with elevated temperatures. The temperature was measured using a thermocouple and was controlled by a proportional–integral–derivative controller.

Separators PTFE-0.1 (Omnipore, hydrophilic, 0.1- $ \mu $ m pore size), PTFE-0.45-thin (Omnipore, hydrophilic, 0.45- $ \mu $ m pore size), PTFE-0.45-thick (Millipore, hydrophilic, 0.45- $ \mu $ m pore size), PTFE-10 (Omnipore, hydrophilic, 10- $ \mu $ m pore size), PVDF-0.45 (Durapore, hydrophilic, 0.45- $ \mu $ m pore size), PES-0.45 (Millipore Express, hydrophilic, 0.45- $ \mu $ m pore size) and PP-0.45 (hydrophilic, 0.45- $ \mu $ m pore size) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PP-Celgard (3419S microporous and 3420 non-woven membrane) was purchased from Celgard. Zirfon-UTP220 was purchased from Agfa. The detailed properties of all these separator materials are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

## Electrochemical measurement

All electrochemical measurements were carried out using an electrochemical workstation (Autolab PGSTA204) connected to a current booster (Metrohm Autolab, 10 A). All voltage was reported directly without current–resistance compensation unless otherwise stated. Gas products were collected directly from the electrolyser cathode outlet using a gas-tight syringe and analysed with a gas chromatograph (PerkinElmer Clarus 590) coupled with a thermal conductivity detector and flame ionization detector. The liquid products were collected

separately from the cathode outlet and anolyte, respectively, and quantified with proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy using a water suppression model, with dimethylsulfoxide as the internal standard.

The following equations were used to determine the FE for the gas and liquid products, respectively:

 $$ \mathsf{F E}_{\mathrm{g a s}}=x_{i}v_{\mathrm{g}}\frac{z_{\mathrm{p},i}F P}{R T}\frac{1}{I_{\mathrm{t o t a l}}}\times100\% $$ 

 $$ \mathsf{FE}_{\mathrm{liquid}}=n_{i}\frac{z_{\mathrm{p},i}F}{Q}\times100\% $$ 

where  $ x_{i} $  represents the volume fraction of gas product i,  $ \nu_{g} $  represents the gas flow rate at the outlet in sccm,  $ z_{p,i} $  represents the number of electrons required to produce one molecule of product i, F represents the Faraday constant, P represents the atmospheric pressure in Pa, R represents the ideal gas constant, T represents the temperature and  $ I_{total} $  represents the total current;  $ n_{i} $  represents the number of moles of product i and Q represents the charged passed while the liquid products are being collected.

EE was calculated as follows:

 $$ \mathsf{E}\mathsf{E}=\sum_{i}^{n}\frac{E_{\mathrm{thermo},i}}{E_{\mathrm{cell}}}\mathsf{F}\mathsf{E}_{i} $$ 

where  $ E_{thermo,i} $  represents the thermodynamic potential of product i and  $ E_{cell} $  represents the full-cell voltage (non-current-resistance-compensated) of the electrolyser.

SPCE was calculated as follows:

 $$ \mathrm{SPCE}=\sum_{i}^{n}\frac{I_{\mathrm{total}}\frac{\mathrm{FE}_{i}}{z_{r,i}F}\times24.5\times10^{3}\left(\frac{\mathrm{cm}^{3}}{\mathrm{mol}}\right)\times60\left(\frac{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{min}}\right)}{v_{\mathrm{CO,input}}} $$ 

where  $ z_{r,i} $  represents the number of electrons required to react one molecule of CO for product i and  $ v_{CO,input} $  represents the CO input flow rate in sccm.

Overpotentials of each electrode component were measured using an Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) reference electrode positioned inline at the anolyte inlet of the electrolyser. The measured potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode scale and current–resistance-corrected using the following equation:

 $$ V(\mathrm{vs\ RHE})=V\left(\mathrm{vs\ Ag/AgCl}\right)+0.205+0.059\mathrm{pH}-iR. $$
