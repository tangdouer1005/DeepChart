Potato late blight, which is caused by Phytophthora infestans and was responsible for the Irish potato famine, remains a major threat to global food security $ ^{1} $ . Most late-blight resistance (R) genes encode nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat proteins (NLRs), but many have been overcome by the rapid evolution of P. infestans $ ^{2} $ . Deploying R genes through hybrid potato breeding $ ^{3-7} $  offers a promising solution to manage late blight. Here we construct a section-wide NLRome comprising 39,211 NLR genes from 31 wild and 21 cultivated potato genomes, representing Solanum section Petota, the tuber-bearing clade. This includes newly sequenced genomes of seven wild species with strong resistance to late blight. Phylogenomic analyses reveal asymmetric patterns of evolution that distinguish sensor and helper NLRs. Mining of the NLRome enabled us to clone Rpi-cph1, a homologue of which has previously been identified only in American black nightshade, and Rpi-cjm1, a Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domain-containing NLR against late blight. We show that non-canonical NLR integrated domains are widespread in the NLRome. Tracing their evolutionary trajectory enabled us to identify Rpi-brk1, an R gene that perceives a P. infestans effector through its heavy-metal-associated (HMA) domain. We find that incorporating the HMA domain into the potato NLR R1 broadens its resistance spectrum, suggesting that a domain ‘plug-in’ strategy could be used to engineer disease resistance. These findings provide a paradigm for R-gene discovery through comparative and evolutionary genomics, and a strategy for R-gene engineering.

Few plant diseases can rival the historical and ongoing impact of late blight. Caused by the oomycete pathogen P. infestans, this disease inflicts up to US 10 billion annually in yield losses and management costs $ ^{8} $ . Late blight triggered the Irish potato famine in the 1840s, leading to severe crop failures and approximately one million deaths $ ^{1,9} $ . Given that potato is the world's leading non-cereal staple crop $ ^{5} $ , efforts have been made to improve potato resistance to late blight by making use of resistance (R) genes from wild relatives $ ^{10} $ . However, rapidly evolving P. infestans populations have overcome most previously identified Resistance to P. infestans (Rpi) genes $ ^{1,10,11} $ , such as R1 (ref. 12), R2 (ref. 13) and R3 (ref. 14). Conventional potato breeding, which focuses on autotetraploid varieties, further complicates the pyramiding of multiple R genes $ ^{15} $  owing to tetrasomic inheritance $ ^{3} $  and limited sexual hybridization. To counter the evolutionary adaptability of P. infestans, hybrid potato breeding has been initiated to transform the potato from a clonally reproduced autotetraploid to a true seed-propagated diploid hybrid, based on inbred lines $ ^{3-5,7} $ . This paradigm shift offers an avenue to accelerate resistance breeding by simplifying genetic analysis with diploid genomes and enabling efficient R-gene stacking through multiple rounds of crossing $ ^{3,7} $ . To realize this potential, discovering additional late-blight R genes is essential.



Most R genes encode NLR proteins $ ^{16} $ , which are categorized into TIR-type NLR (TIR-NLRs), coiled-coil (CC)-type NLR (CNLs) and RESISTANCE TO POWDERY MILDEW 8 (RPW8) CC domain-containing NLR (RNLs) $ ^{17} $  on the basis of domain architecture. Some NLRs contain non-canonical integrated domains (IDs) that resemble pathogen targets $ ^{16} $ . NLRs can sense pathogen-secreted effectors directly or indirectly, triggering immune responses that often lead to a hypersensitive response (HR), a form of localized cell death at the infection site $ ^{18-20} $ . Functionally, they can act as 'sensors' (pathogen recognition), 'helpers' (immune signalling) and 'singletons' (performing both sensor and helper roles) $ ^{16,18,21} $ . Previous research has classified NLRs into type I and type II, with distinct evolutionary trajectories $ ^{22} $ . Type I NLRs are moderately related homologues, with no obvious allelic or orthologous relationships among species, whereas their type II counterparts have a single representative

from each species $ ^{22} $ . The biological significance of this divergence, however, remains elusive.

Previous studies have cloned nearly 30 Rpi genes from Solanum species, all encoding CNLs $ ^{10,11,23,24} $ . Resistance gene enrichment sequencing (RenSeq) has been developed and integrated with other genetic approaches for the rapid identification of R genes $ ^{25-27} $ . The recent accumulation of genomic data has enabled systematic examination of NLR diversity and a deeper understanding of NLR evolution, as shown in pepper $ ^{28} $ , Arabidopsis $ ^{29} $ , wild tomato $ ^{30} $  and rice $ ^{31} $ . However, the translation of NLR polymorphisms and evolutionary patterns into functional R-gene discovery remains limited. Here, we report a section-wide inventory of 39,211 NLRs from 52 wild and cultivated potato accessions representing 25 species. Comparative and evolutionary genomic analyses enabled the identification of three NLRs that confer resistance to late blight. These findings will inform the genome design of diploid potato hybrids with durable resistance to late blight, and our methodology will support the experimental investigation of R-gene mining in other plant species.

## Genomes of late-blight-resistant potato

Other than the release of the DM1-3 516 R44 (hereafter referred to as DM) potato reference genome $ ^{32-34} $  and genome assemblies of 24 wild and 20 cultivated diploid potato accessions $ ^{35} $ , genomes of wild potato species with high resistance to P. infestans are scarce. We reasoned that combining in silico genomic analyses with functional screening in late-blight-resistant wild potatoes could facilitate the discovery of new R genes. Using detached leaf assays, we identified seven late-blight-resistant wild potato species (Solanum candolleanum, Solanum cardiophyllum, Solanum cajamarquense, Solanum chacoense, Solanum commersonii, Solanum dolichocremastrum and Solanum hintonii; Supplementary Table 1), representing all three phylogenetic taxa of wild potatoes (clades 1 + 2, clade 3 and clade 4) $ ^{36} $ . We performed Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) high-fidelity (HiFi) sequencing and generated an average of 25.4 Gb reads per species (around 30× coverage; Supplementary Table 2). Monoploid assembly length ranged from 666.7 to 976.9 Mb, with a mean contig N50 of 19.7 Mb and a base accuracy of 99.9%. Benchmarking universal single-copy orthologues (BUSCO) $ ^{37} $  confirmed 94.6–98.3% completeness of embryophyte single-copy genes. These assemblies also achieved 'reference' level, evidenced by a mean long-terminal repeat (LTR) assembly index $ ^{38} $  of 12.3, indicating high continuity within the repetitive regions (Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3). We annotated 33,517–40,103 protein-coding genes by integrating transcriptomic evidence, homology-based searches and ab initio predictions (Extended Data Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 4–6). Collectively, these high-quality assemblies provide accurate, continuous and complete genomic resources, establishing a robust genomic platform for R-gene discovery in potatoes.

## Section-wide NLRome of Petota

A detailed understanding of R-gene diversity and evolution in the tuber-bearing section  $ Petota $  requires a comprehensive NLR repertoire. To capture this diversity, we selected 52 high-quality monoploid genome assemblies—7 from this study, 44 from Tang et al. $ ^{35} $  and the DM reference genome $ ^{32,33} $ —representing the diversity and phylogeny of wild and cultivated potato species (Supplementary Fig. 1). We reannotated 39,211 NLR-encoding genes across these genomes, with copy numbers ranging from 348 to 1,105 (Methods, Supplementary Fig. 2, Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Note 1). NLR copy number showed no significant correlation with monoploid genome size (Extended Data Fig. 1c).

On the basis of phylogenetic relationships and sequence similarities, we constructed the  $ Petota\ NLRome $  by clustering the 39,211 NLRs into 6 NLR super clades $ ^{39} $ : TIR-NLRs, RNLs,  $ CC_{G10} $ -NLRs $ ^{40} $ , CC-NLR required for cell death (NRC)-helpers, CC-NRC-sensors and CC-NLR-others. These were further resolved into 489 clades (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11 and Supplementary Fig. 3). We found that conserved NLR clades represent only 24.1% of the 489 clades; the remaining NLR clades varied across the 52 potato accessions (Supplementary Fig. 4). One notable example is the absence of the  $ NRCX(\text{ref. 41}) $  orthologue in all six accessions from S. tuberosum Group Stenotomum (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The self-incompatible nature of most diploid potatoes has led to high heterozygosity within their genomes. Among the 49 heterozygous potato genomes, we observed an average NLR haplotype diversity of 8.4%, with landrace accessions showing significantly higher diversity than Mexican wild potato species (clades 1 + 2; Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6).



We next classified these NLR clades into type I and type II on the basis of branch length patterns in phylogenetic trees and allelic or orthologous relationships, following a previous study $ ^{22} $ . Most (92.8%) clades were type II, similar to Arabidopsis $ ^{29} $ , but these accounted for only 32.1% of NLR genes. The remaining 35 (7.2%) were type I, representing 67.1% of all NLRs and showing significantly higher copy numbers across the 52 potato genomes. By contrast, most type II clades had only zero to two copies per genome (Fig. 1). The haplotype diversity of type I NLRs is considerably higher than that of type II NLRs (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Notably, 10 of the 11 helper NLR homologues were found in type II clades, with NRC4 (ref. 21) being the only exception (Extended Data Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 11). Seventeen out of the 18 (94.4%) previously characterized sensor NLRs that recognize pathogen effectors from oomycetes, nematodes and viruses had potato homologues in type I clades. By contrast, the potato homologues of eight out of nine sensor NLRs recognizing bacterial and fungal pathogens belonged to type II clades (Fig. 1, Extended Data Fig. 2e, Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Note 4). These findings suggest that highly dynamic type I NLRs function mainly as sensors of rapidly evolving pathogens, whereas helper NLRs, which mediate downstream immune signalling $ ^{21} $ , seem to have experienced fewer duplications. The NLRome described here provides a framework to characterize and exploit R-gene diversity in wild and cultivated potatoes.

### Cloning Rpi-cph1 from S. cardiophyllum

Solanum americanum, a non-tuber-bearing wild species, exhibits high resistance to late blight and is considered as a non-host $ ^{24} $ . Several Rpi genes, such as Rpi-amr1 and Rpi-amr3, have been cloned from this species $ ^{23,24} $ . However, reproductive barriers impede their direct use in potato breeding. An alternative approach is to identify functional homologues of these genes in wild potato species that can cross with cultivated potatoes. We found that NLRs in the type I NLR clade, Clade006, are homologous to Rpi-amr3, suggesting the presence of functional Rpi-amr3 homologues in potato (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Within this clade, we identified a cluster of NB-ARC-domain-containing genes, with copy numbers markedly reduced in clades 1 + 2 wild species compared with other potatoes (Fig. 2a,b and Supplementary Table 12). In this cluster, only one gene was annotated as a full-length CNL (NLR_OOG005810_1_CNL) in S. cardiophyllum, a clade 1 + 2 Mexican wild potato species highly resistant to P. infestans (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 7).

Genome alignment and PCR amplification indicated the presence of two alleles, Rpi-cph1.1 and Rpi-cph1.2. Transient expression of either allele in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves conferred resistance against P. infestans isolate JH19 (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Stable transformation of the susceptible potato S. tuberosum cv. Désirée (lines 10, 13 and 17 for Rpi-cph1.1 and 7, 16 and 22 for Rpi-cph1.2) confirmed their considerable resistance to five P. infestans isolates, which was abolished in N. benthamiana leaves in which nrc2, nrc3 and nrc4 were knocked out (nrc2/3/4 knockout) (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 3d–g).

Given the clear single-copy nature of Rpi-cjm1 orthologues across 52 wild and cultivated potatoes (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12), we transiently expressed 10 orthologues in N. benthamiana leaves, which revealed resistance to JH19 in alleles from S. cajamarquense, S. sogarandinum and S. cardiophyllum (Supplementary Fig. 13a–d). Among them, S. sogarandinum has an endosperm balance number of 2 (2EBN), identical to that of cultivated potato, which suggests interspecific crossability $ ^{49} $ . Therefore, the Rpi-cjm1 orthologue from S. sogarandinum holds immense potential for potato breeding and commercial application.

## I D evolution in potato NL Rome

Acquisition of non-canonical IDs in NLRs, which mimic bona fide effector targets, enables pathogen recognition $ ^{16} $ . However, the diversity and evolution of IDs in potato NLRs remain poorly understood. We identified 1,495 ID-containing NLRs (3.8% of 39,211) across 52 potato accessions, encompassing 165 distinct IDs (Extended Data Fig. 5a and Supplementary Tables 13 and 14). Of these, 31 IDs were unique to wild potato species from clades 3 + 4, and 19 IDs were shared across all 52 accessions (Extended Data Fig. 6a). The most common was the HMA domain, present in 119 NLRs from 46 accessions (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Other frequent IDs included RVT_2, analogous to soybean SRZ4 that confers resistance against six viruses $ ^{50} $ . Some IDs, such as P450 and PIF1, were not detected in other species such as Arabidopsis $ ^{29} $ ; further experimental research is needed to understand their roles in plant immunity.

NLRs with IDs (ID-NLRs) were unevenly distributed across phylogenetic clades: the  $ CC_{G10} $ -NLR clade contained the highest proportion (34.7%), whereas none were found in the RNL clade, which includes the helper NLRs ADR1 and NRG1 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). ID-NLRs with the same domain tended to cluster within closely related phylogenetic branches. Notably, all 119 HMA-NLRs grouped with R1 (Extended Data Fig. 6b), a CC-NLR-type late-blight-resistance gene in potato $ ^{12} $ .



IDs might have been integrated into plant NLRs as decoys because pathogen-derived molecules repeatedly target them $ ^{51} $ ; however, whether these IDs originate from the genomes themselves remains mostly unknown. Most (150 out of 165) NLR-encoded IDs also occurred in non-NLR genes within potato genomes, (Extended Data Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 15). Compared with their non-NLR counterparts, IDs in NLRs exhibited higher  $ K_{a}/K_{s} $  ratios and lower Tajima's D scores, consistent with weaker purifying selection (Extended Data Fig. 5e,f and Supplementary Table 16). The HMA domain, in particular, showed particularly strong positive selection in potato NLRs ( $ K_{a}/K_{s} = 1.74 $  in NLRs versus 0.43 in non-NLR genes; Extended Data Fig. 5e,f).

To trace the origin of HMA integration, we examined 94 solanaceous genomes $ ^{52} $ , and detected NLRs with HMA (NLR-HMAs) in 42 species (Extended Data Fig. 5g). All wild and cultivated potatoes (except for S. andreanum) carried NLR-HMAs, whereas no such genes were found in their sister species, tomato (Solanum section Lycopersicon). NLR-HMAs also occurred in other Solanum species such as S. etubero-sum (a potato-like, non-tuber-bearing species); S. americanum, a wild nightshade containing several Rpi genes $ ^{23} $ ; and Solanum anguivi, a close wild relative of cultivated aubergine. Outside Solanum, however, only Jaltomata sinuosa carried a single NLR-HMA (Extended Data Fig. 5g). Notably, all Solanaceae NLR-HMAs were close homologues of R1 (Extended Data Fig. 7), with HMA consistently integrated at the C terminus. Phylogenetic analysis suggested a common Solanum ancestor for NLR-HMA (Extended Data Fig. 8a,b). Meanwhile, all NLR-HMAs were located in the R1 gene cluster (Extended Data Fig. 9). These results indicate that the acquisition of HMA in NLRs is likely to have been an ancient event that predated the early divergence of Solanum.

## Rpi-brk1 requires HMA for resistance

The widespread integration of HMA domains in NLRs across both wild and cultivated potatoes, together with evidence of positive selection, prompted us to investigate whether NLR-HMAs confer resistance to potato diseases, such as late blight. We transiently expressed 12 NLR-HMAs from 8 wild potato species (Solanum burkartii, S. candolleanum, S. cardiophyllum, S. cajamarquense, S. chacoense, Solanum chomatophilum, S. commersonii and S. sogarandinum) in N. benthamiana leaves, followed by inoculation with P. infestans. The NLR-HMA3 from S. burkartii showed robust resistance to late blight (Supplementary Fig. 14a). To verify this, we generated stable transgenic lines of S. tuberosum cv. Désirée expressing NLR-HMA3. Three independent lines (1, 3 and 9) showed resistance to the P. infestans isolate 88069, and four additional isolates in detached leaf assays (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 14e). Therefore, we designated this gene Rpi-brk1.

Screening the P. infestans effector library $ ^{42} $  revealed that PITG_20616, an RxLR effector with secretory activity, triggered cell death when co-expressed with Rpi-brk1 in N. benthamiana leaves; thus, we named it AVRbrk1 (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Figs. 8c,e and 14b and Supplementary Note 7). Rpi-brk1-induced cell death in response to AVRbrk1 and its resistance to P. infestans were abolished in nrc2/3/4-knockout N. benthamiana (Fig. 4c,d and Extended Data Fig. 10a,b). BN-PAGE assays further showed that Rpi-brk1 and AVRbrk1 caused strong oligomerization of StNRC4 in nrc2/3/4-knockout N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 4e). These results indicate that Rpi-brk1-mediated resistance depends on NRC helpers (Supplementary Fig. 9c).

## Plug-in for resistance engineering

To examine whether  $ HMA^{brk1} $  can expand the recognition spectrum of other potato NLRs, we fused  $ HMA^{brk1} $  to the C terminus of several R genes. Co-expression assays showed that R1 fused with  $ HMA^{brk1} $  (R1– $ HMA^{brk1} $ ) gained the ability to recognize AVRbrk1 while retaining its recognition of AVR1 (Fig. 5a). However, neither R3a– $ HMA^{brk1} $  nor Rpi-blb2– $ HMA^{brk1} $  triggered cell death when co-expressed with AVRbrk1. Of note, co-expressing R3a– $ HMA^{brk1} $  with AVR3a did not trigger cell death in N. benthamiana, whereas Rpi-blb2– $ HMA^{brk1} $  retained AVRblb2 recognition. R8-HMA $ ^{brk1} $  even caused auto-activation in N. benthamiana leaves. We also integrated HMA domains from a dysfunctional NLR (NLR-HMA11 of S. cajamarquense,  $ HMA^{cjm} $ ; Supplementary Fig. 14a) and from a non-NLR protein (HMA $ ^{nonNLR} $ ) into these four tested NLRs (Supplementary Table 17), but none of these recognized AVRbrk1 (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 14f). These results suggest that HMA $ ^{brk1} $ -mediated effector recognition is restricted to the R1-like NLR clade rather than being broadly transferable.

The R1 gene, discovered in 2002, has been widely used to breed resistance to late blight in potato $ ^{12} $ , but its effectiveness has been compromised by AVR1 mutations in P. infestans $ ^{10} $ . We showed that transgenic potato lines expressing the engineered R1-HMA $ ^{brk1} $ , but

not  $ R1-HMA^{brk1}-m7 $ , conferred disease resistance against P. infestans strain NLO7434, which carries AVRbrk1 but lacks AVR1 (Fig. 5b,c and Supplementary Fig. 14c,d). These findings suggest that  $ HMA^{brk1} $  serves as a 'plug-in' module to broaden the resistance spectrum of R1 homologues, providing a promising strategy for engineering late-blight resistance in potato.

## Discussion

We have constructed a comprehensive repertoire of R genes for the tuber-bearing section Petota. The seven newly sequenced, late-blight-resistant wild potato genomes presented here will enable further use of diploid sources of late-blight resistance in potato breeding. A previous study examined NLRs from 45 potato genomes, suggesting a co-evolution between the expansion of the potato NLR repertoire and the emergence of underground organs, such as tubers $ ^{35} $ . By integrating our 7 new genomes with these 45 published genomes, we were able to link the evolutionary dynamics of potato R genes to their functional roles in immunity. Sensor NLRs are abundant among the rapidly evolving type I NLRs, which are characterized by large and variable paralogues. Conversely, most helper NLRs fall into the more conserved type II category, showing allelic relationships and low copy number variation. These patterns could not be observed by analysing only the seven genomes, emphasizing the power of large-scale comparative genomics. Furthermore, we found that the two types of sensor NLR are closely linked to the recognition of two groups of pathogens: type INLRs (oomycetes, nematodes and viruses) and type II NLRs (bacteria and fungi) (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2e), possibly reflecting adaptation to pathogens with different evolutionary potentials $ ^{2} $ .

Pathogenic selection pressure during plant–pathogen co-evolution is generally considered a major driving force in NLR evolution $ ^{17,19} $ . P. infestans, the late-blight pathogen, probably originated in Mexico, where extensive co-evolution with local wild potato species belonging to clades 1 + 2 (ref. 36) has occurred $ ^{9,10,54} $ . Indeed, most cloned Rpi genes come from these Mexican species $ ^{10} $ , underscoring their importance as sources of resistance. Some Rpi genes found in South American wild potatoes might also have resistant homologues in these species, such as Rpi-cjm1, which we identified in this study (Supplementary Fig. 13). Our NLRome shows that these Mexican wild potato species possess significantly fewer NLRs than do South American clade 3 + 4 species or landraces (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 7). This reduced repertoire might facilitate Rpi gene mining by reducing the complexity of functional validation. Subsequently, the identified Rpi genes, such as Rpi-cph1 in this study, could be introduced into potato cultivars through bridge crossing or embryo rescue techniques $ ^{55} $ , as shown by the successful introgression of Rpi-blb1 from the wild potato S. bulbocastanum into cultivated potato $ ^{56} $ . These approaches can help to bypass the costly deregulation that is associated with genetic transformation.

When fusing the functional HMA domain (HMA $ ^{brk1} $ ) to other NLRs, only R1–HMA $ ^{brk1} $  gained the HMA-derived effector recognition capability (Fig. 5a). Together with the evolutionarily constrained integration of HMA into R1 homologues, these results highlight a 'compatibility' between IDs and NLRs—a key consideration for the rational engineering of resistance. Given the strict regulations and societal barriers around transgenic crops, we propose that the ID 'plug-in' strategy, in which functional IDs are integrated into NLRs in situ through genome editing, has the potential to confer disease resistance. This approach can generate marker-free, biosafe plant lines that address public concerns $ ^{57} $ . Realizing this strategy will depend on the development of efficient knock-in technologies in potato.

## Samples and sequencing

Seven wild potato accessions were selected for genome sequencing on the basis of detached leaf assays (DLA) screening (Supplementary Table 1): S. candolleanum, S. cardiophyllum, S. cajamarquense, S. chacoense, S. commersonii, S. dolichocremastrum, and S. hintonii. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. PacBio HiFi sequencing was used with the circular consensus sequencing mode for these seven accessions on the Sequel II platform. We used the CCS program (v.6.2.0, https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) to calculate the consensus among the sequenced subreads, generating 20.8–30.6 Gb of HiFi reads, which represented 25.9-fold (C509) to 38.3-fold (C534) of genome coverages (Supplementary Table 2). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) libraries of the seven accessions were constructed by extracting the total RNA from the four mixed tissues of roots, stems, leaves and flowers, followed by sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system to generate around 5 Gb data for each accession (Supplementary Table 3).

## Genome assembly

Contigs of the seven accessions were assembled using Hifiasm (v.0.16.1-r375) $ ^{59} $  with the ‘--primary’ parameter. Hifiasm, under the parameter set herein, produced both primary and alternate assemblies, the primary one of which might still contain unresolved haplotigs, representing the heterozygous regions in the genome. To retain only the haploid content of the initial genome assembly, we applied purge_dups (v.1.2.5) $ ^{60} $  to remove putative haplotigs from the primary assembly, with the default parameters. We used Merqury (v.1.3) $ ^{61} $  to estimate the base-level accuracy (quality value; QV) of our assemblies, with a k-mer size of 19. QV equals  $ -10 \times \log_{10}(P) $ , where P is the probability of an incorrect assembled base. HMM-Flagger (v.1.1.0) $ ^{62} $  was used to evaluate the proportion of correctly assembled regions based on the mapping coverage of long reads. The windowLen parameter in HMM-Flagger was set to 16,000, optimized for PacBio HiFi data. The completeness in genic regions was evaluated by BUSCO v.5.2.2 (ref. 37) on the basis of the embryophyta_odb10 database, using the ‘--augustus’ parameter. The assembly quality of intergenic space and repetitive elements was estimated using LAI (v.2.9.0) $ ^{38} $ , by assessing the completeness of intact LTR retrotransposons.

## Repeat element analyses

Transposable elements (TEs), in terms of LTR, terminal inverted repeat and helitron, were identified using the Extensive de-novo TE Annotator (EDTA, v.1.9.4) $ ^{63} $ . The non-redundant repeat libraries output from EDTA was then passed to RepeatMasker v.1.332 (http://www.repeatmasker.org) for repeat masking, using the default parameters.

## Prediction of protein-coding genes

Prediction of protein-coding genes was performed using a uniform annotation pipeline. We used HISAT2 v.2.2.2 (ref. 64) to map RNA-seq reads against the assembled genome with the ‘--dta’ parameter, followed by transcript assembling with the guide of genome by StringTie v.1.1.3 (ref. 65) with default parameters. These results were considered as hints and were then fed into BRAKER (v.2.1.5) $ ^{66} $  (with parameters ‘--nocleanup --softmasking’) to train AUGUSTUS v.3.3.3 (https://github.com/Gaius-Augustus/Augustus) and GeneMark-ET (v.4.68_lic) $ ^{67} $  for ab initio gene prediction. The hidden Markov model within SNAP (v.2006-07-28) $ ^{68} $  was trained by passing the candidate transcript assemblies generated from the PASA pipeline (v.2.3.3) $ ^{69} $ .

To generate a non-redundant homologous protein dataset to facilitate gene prediction, we downloaded the UniProt Swiss-Prot datasets containing high-confidence plant protein sequences (https://www.uniprot.org/downloads), in conjunction with the previously reported amino acid sequences of tomato and potato $ ^{32,33,70,71} $ . These sequences were merged and redundancy was removed by cd-hit-est (ref. 72) with the default parameters. Gene structures predicted by AUGUSTUS, GeneMark-ET and SNAP, homologous proteins and transcript assemblies were passed into MAKER v.2.31.11 (ref. 73) to synthesize and polish the final gene models. We used InterProScan v.5.53-87.0 (ref. 74) to predict functional domains with parameters '-cli-iprlookup -tsv -applPfam'.



## Reannotation of nucleotide-binding resistance genes

Nucleotide-binding resistance genes exist mainly as gene clusters with high levels of sequence similarity, hindering their prediction. We implemented an 'NLR local annotation' pipeline, as applied by Tang et al. $ ^{[35]} $ , to reannotate NLR-encoding genes in the 52 potato accessions (7 in this study, 44 in Tang et al. $ ^{[35]} $  and the potato reference line DM), 8 wild tomato species (Solanum chmielewskii, Solanum habrochaites, Solanum pennellii, Solanum corneliomulleri, Solanum peruvianum, Solanum galapagense, Solanum neorickii and Solanum pimpinellifolium) $ ^{[75]} $  and one cultivated tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). NLR reannotation was also applied to the Solanaceae genomes used in a previous study $ ^{[52]} $ . We used NLR-Annotator (v.0.7) $ ^{[76]} $  with the default parameters to extract genomic regions containing putative NLR genes. To specifically facilitate the prediction of NLRs, we generated a NLR protein homologue dataset comprising 7,007 amino acid sequences from a previous study $ ^{[30]} $ , putative NLR genes in Arabidopsis (annotation version Araprot11) $ ^{[77]} $ , and experiment-validated NLR genes obtained from PRGdb 3.0 (ref. 78) and RefPlantNLR (ref. 39). The genomic fragments containing putative NLRs and their 2-kb left and right flanking sequences were extracted for NLR prediction. NLR homologous proteins served as the homology evidence, and the training sets of SNAP, AUGUSTUS and GeneMark-ET, and assembled transcripts described in the section 'Prediction of protein-coding genes', provided the de novo gene prediction structure and expression evidence, respectively. These were fed into MAKER for annotation of NLR gene models. The predicted NLRs were then classified into three major classes according to their domain and motif structures by NLRtracker (v.1.01) $ ^{[39]} $ : TIR-NLR (containing at least a TIR domain), CNL (at least a CC domain is present) and NL (containing at least a nucleotide-binding site (NB-ARC) but with neither TIR nor CC present). They were further categorized into eight minor classes: CNL (CC, NB-ARC and leucine-rich repeat (LRR)), CN (CC and NB-ARC), CCX (CC only), TIR-NLR (TIR, NB-ARC and LRR), TN (TIR and NB-ARC), TX (TIR only), NL (NB-ARC and LRR) and NBS (NB-ARC only). Because the NLR-annotator tends to fail to retrieve the RESISTANCE TO POWDERY MILDEW 8 (RPW8)−NB-ARC−LRR (RNL) subclass of NLRs $ ^{[76]} $ , we identified RNLs from our whole-genome gene predictions and manually integrated them with the NLR-annotator-based NLR prediction set. NLRs with only RPW8 and NB-ARC domains were not observed in the above-mentioned genomes. The resulting NLR gene prediction was integrated with the original whole-genome gene annotation.

## I dentification of paired NLRs and NLR clusters

Paired NLRs were defined as a pair of adjacent NLRs arranged in a head-to-head orientation, in which the first NLR was in the minus strand and the second one was in the plus strand. NLR clusters were defined as a series of NLRs (at least three members) that distributed sufficiently proximal to each other ( $ \leq $ 200 kb), with no more than eight non-NLR genes present in this region, as suggested previously $ ^{79} $ .

## Calculation of Tajima's $D$, nucleotide diversity and $K_{a}/K_{s}$

Nucleotide coding sequences among NLR-encoding genes in NLR clades with members present in at least four accessions were extracted and aligned using MAFFT (v.7.490) $ ^{80} $  with the ‘--auto’ parameter. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified from the resulting alignment using an in-house Python script. Multi-allelic records were split into bi-allelic sites using the ‘norm’ command of BCFtools (v.1.13) $ ^{81} $  with parameters ‘-m -any’. Tajima’s D statistics were computed using VCFtools (v.0.1.16) $ ^{82} $  by the ‘--TajimaD 1’ parameter.

Nucleotide diversity in terms of  $ \pi $  was calculated using the ‘--site-pi’ parameter. The average values of all sites were regarded as the Tajima's D or nucleotide diversity for a given NLR clade. The significance test was applied using a two-tailed Wilcoxon rank sum test by the 'Wilcox. exact' function (default parameters) from the R package exactRankTests (v.0.8-35). To compute  $ K_{a}/K_{s} $ , codon alignment within each NLR clade was first performed using ParaAT (v.2.0) $ ^{83} $ , with parameters ‘-m muscle -f axt -k’. Phylogenetic trees within each NLR clade were inferred using NB domain amino acid alignment by RAxML (v.8.2.12) $ ^{84} $  with 100 bootstraps, setting the parameters to ‘-f a -x 5 -p 5 -#100 -m PROTGAM-MAJTT’. The codeml program in the PAML package (v.4.9) $ ^{85} $  was then applied to estimate  $ K_{a}/K_{s} $  within each NLR clade based on the ‘one’ model for codons; the other parameters were set to default.

## Phylogenetic analyses

We used OrthoFinder (v.2.5.4) $ ^{86} $  to build a species tree to compute orthologue groups among the predicted protein-coding genes of the 52 potato accessions. Global alignment of amino acid sequences from these genes was applied using MAFFT (v.7.490) $ ^{80} $ , with the ‘--auto’ parameter. IQ-TREE v.2.0.6 (ref. 87) was used to infer a maximum-likelihood phylogeny by automatically computing the best-fit amino acid substitution model, via ‘-m MFP’ parameter. The bootstrap value was set to 100 to produce a consensus tree topology.

For constructing phylogeny among NLRs, amino acid sequences of NB-ARC domains were extracted according to the output from HMMER (v.3.3.2) $ ^{88} $ , setting the E-value threshold to 0.001. Multiple alignments were generated using MAFFT (v.7.490) $ ^{80} $  with the ‘--auto’ parameter. RAxML (v.8.2.12) $ ^{84} $  inferred maximum-likelihood phylogenetic relationships with 100 bootstraps, setting the parameters to ‘-f a -x 5 -p 5 -# 100 -m PROTGAMMAJTT’. The best-fit amino acid substitution model (JTT) was chosen by running IQ-TREE v.2.0.6 (ref. 87) using the parameter ‘-m MF’.

## Construction and analyses of the NL Rome

Using the above-mentioned approaches, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed for the predicted NLRs from the 52 potato accessions. These NLRs were further classified into six major clades: RNLs, TIR-NLRs,  $ CC_{G10} $ -NLRs, CC-NLR-others, CC-NRC-helpers and CC-NRC-sensors, on the basis of their phylogeny positions and previously reported NLRs, as suggested previously $ ^{[39]} $ . We further classified the 6 NLR super clades into 489 clades by manually examining the phylogenetic tree (members in a given clade share a common ancestor). Those without NB-ARC domains were added to existing clades or new clades according to the clustering results of OrthoFinder (v.2.5.4; default parameters) $ ^{[86]} $ . We then computed the number of CNLs, TIR-NLRs, NILs and RNLs in each NLR clade and assigned a clade to CNL-clade, TIR-NLR-clade, NL-clade or RNL-clade according to the most prevalent type of its NLR members. If more than 50% of the members of an NLR clade were classified as paired or clustered NLRs, this clade was regarded as a paired or clustered clade. Single-copy NLR clades were defined as those containing only one member in more than 48 of the 52 (95%) potato accessions. NLR clades belonging to type I or type II were determined according to the branch length of their phylogeny and allelic relationships among potato accessions, as described previously $ ^{[22]} $ . To build a phylogenetic tree for the 489 NL-Rome clades, the longest NLR with a NB-ARC domain in each clade was selected as the representative. The phylogeny was then inferred using RAxML (v.8.2.12; see the aforementioned parameters) based on the amino acid alignment of NB-ARC domains. To simplify visualization, NLR clades whose mean branch length relative to their leaves is less than one are collapsed using iTOL (v.5.1) $ ^{[89]} $ . Simulation of pan- and core-NL-Rome sizes in terms of the number of NLR clades was performed using PanGP (v.1.01) $ ^{[90]} $  by a totally random algorithm. The number of combinations was set to 500 at each number of incorporated genomes, and the sampling process was replicated 100 times.

## I dentification of NLR haplotype variants

For the 49 heterozygous potato accessions (excluding A6-26, E4-63 and DM), PacBio HiFi reads were mapped against their respective genome assemblies using minimap2 (v.2.21) $ ^{91} $  with the '-ax map-hifi' parameter. SNP and indel (<30 bp) variants were identified using BCFtools (v.1.13 $ ^{81} $  in PacBio-CCS mode, with multi-allelic and rare-variant calling enabled. To filter out low-quality variants, the following criteria were applied: QUAL < 10 || MQ < 10 || DP < 3 || MQBZ < -(3.5 + 4*DP/QUAL) || RPBZ > (3 + 3*DP/QUAL) || RPBZ < -(3 + 3*DP/QUAL) || FORMAT/SP > (40 + DP/2) || SCBZ > (2.5 + DP/30). For structural variant (SV;  $ \geq $ 30 bp) calling, HiFi alignments were fed into cuteSV (v.2.1.0) $ ^{92} $  using the following parameters: '-max cluster_bias_INS 1000 --diff_ratio_merging_lns 0.9 --max_cluster_bias_DEL 1000 --diff_ratio_merging_DEL 0.5 --min_support 4 --min_size 30 --max_size 100000'. SVs tagged as 'IMPRECISE' were excluded, and only heterozygous SNPs, indels and SVs were retained. For each accession, NLR haplotype diversity was calculated as the total affected length of SNPs, indels and SVs within NLRs divided by the total length of NLR genes.

## I dentification of non-canonical IDs

Putative protein domains were predicted using the script pfam_scan.pl (https://github.com/jlli6t/pfam_scan). Non-canonical domains that have been integrated into NLRs were identified after filtering out the canonical domains present in NLRs, which comprise NB-ARC, NB-LRR, NLR_C4, LRR, TIR, Rx_N and RPW8, following the reported pipeline (https://github.com/krasileva-group/plant_rgenes) $ ^{93} $ . The E-value threshold was set to 0.05. IDs present in only one NLR and one accession were excluded to mitigate the effect of annotation artefacts. To calculate Tajima's D and  $ K_{a}/K_{s} $ , only IDs present in at least four accessions in both NLR and non-NLR genes were considered.

## I dentification of significantly contracted NLR clades in clades 1 + 2 potato species

The 52 potato accessions were divided into two categories: clades 1 + 2 potato species and other potato accessions. In each NLR clade from the NLRome, a non-parametric test between NLR copy number in clades 1 + 2 potato species and other potatoes was performed by Wilcoxon rank sum test using the 'wilcox.exact' function in the R package 'exactRankTests' (v.0.8-35, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/exactRankTests/index.html) with default parameters. Those with P < 0.01 were considered as NLR clades that have undergone copy-number contraction in clades 1 + 2 potato species, compared with other potato accessions.

## Synteny analyses

The genome-wide gene syntenic relationship between two given potato genomes was determined using GeneTribe (v.1.2.0) $ ^{94} $ . The synteny plot was generated using a customized R script (https://github.com/HongboDoll/PotatoNLRome/blob/main/Clades12%2034%20NLR%20clade%20comparison/NLR%20gene%20synteny%20plot/card1_8_potato_synteny.R).

## GWAS

A total of 101 diploid wild potato accessions, representing 34 species, were screened for HR using 213 core effectors that are conserved in P. infestans. Among them, 50 accessions (21 species) responded to at least one effector or the selected positive control NPP1. SNPs and indels of these 50 accessions showing missing genotype call frequencies greater than 0.1 or minor allele frequencies less than 0.05 were excluded; the remaining ones were used to calculate a kinship matrix using the 'emmax-kin' program with the '-d10' parameter in the EMMAX software (v.20120205) $ ^{95} $ . The first ten principal components were computed using SNPs and indels with linkage disequilibrium pruned (plink parameters '-indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2'). Association tests

were performed using EMMAX $ ^{95} $  incorporating the 206,330 SNPs and indels passing quality control, co-variants (principal components) and kinship. The genome-wide significance threshold  $ (9.12 \times 10^{-6}) $  was determined from the Bonferroni correction threshold of 1/n, where n is the effective number of independent SNPs and indels calculated using the Genetic Type I error calculator  $ (v0.2)^{96} $ . The identified association (PITG_04085) was found in 19 out of the 50 potato accessions.

## Gene-expression profiling

After quality control using fastp (v.0.23.2) $ ^{97} $  with the default parameters, previously reported RNA-seq reads of 20 potato accessions $ ^{35} $  for various tissues and transcriptomic sequencing data of the 7 high-resistance potato accessions were mapped to the corresponding assembled genome using HISAT2 (v.2.2.2) (ref. 64), with parameters '-x --dta'. Genome-guided transcript assembly was performed using StringTie (v.1.1.3) $ ^{65} $ , with the '-G' parameter. The expression levels of these transcripts were computed in terms of transcripts per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (TPM) values. Those showing TPM values greater than 1 were regarded as expressed NLRs.

## Effectoromics response analysis

A total of 212 RxLR effectors without signal peptide regions from the P. infestans reference genome (isolate T30-4) were cloned into PVX vector (pGR107). All pGR107-derived constructs were independently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 as the effector library for effectoromics screening. All diploid potato plants were grown in environment-controlled growth chambers under long-day conditions (16-h light-8-h dark cycle at 140  $ \mu $ E s $ ^{-1} $  m $ ^{-2} $  light intensity) at 22 °C. Leaves from four- or five-week-old potato plants were used for agroinfection, and cell death phenotypes were recorded from 0 (no HR) to 10 (strong HR) ten days after inoculation. At least nine leaves from three independent plants were used for each assay.

## Plasmid constructs for transient gene-expression analysis

To verify the functions of candidate NLR or effector genes, open reading frames of the selected genes were PCR-amplified by 2× Phanta Max Master Mix (Vazyme, P515), and cloned into binary vector pBin307 or pBin308 with the CaMV 35S promoter and CaMV 35 terminator by SmaI (NEB, R0141) site, or binary vector pBinGFP2 (GFP-tagged) with the CaMV 35S promoter and the CaMV 35 terminator by SmaI site, or pPZP-RCS2 with the selected gene's native operons by I-CeuI (NEB, R0699), I-Scel (NEB, R0694) and I-AscI (NEB, R0558) sites. The sequencing-verified plasmid constructs were then transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 for transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. All plasmid constructs and primers are listed in Supplementary Table 19.

## Plant growth and transformation

The diploid potato accessions were preserved under in vitro conditions as tissue-culture plants. The three- to four-week-old potato seedlings were transferred into pots with sterilized, moist soil and grown in an environment-controlled growth chamber under long-day conditions (16-h light-8-h dark cycle, at a light intensity of  $ 140 \mu E s^{-1} m^{-2} $  and 45–65% humidity) at  $ 22^{\circ} C $ . After four to five weeks, the potato plants were used for the HR or inoculation assays.

For N. benthamiana, plants were sown and grown in another environment-controlled growth chamber under long-day conditions (16-h light–8-h dark cycle at  $ 140 \mu E s^{-1} m^{-2} $  light intensity, 65–80% humidity) at  $ 25^{\circ}C $ . Four- or five-week-old plants were used for agro-infiltration or HR assays.

For the potato stable transformation experiments, stem segments of the potato variety Désirée were cut from in vitro plants and placed on solid MS20 medium (0.88 g l $ ^{-1} $  Murashige and Skoog medium powder, PhytoTech Labs M516; 20 g l $ ^{-1} $  sucrose; 8 g l $ ^{-1} $  agar) with 2,4-D (1 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) and kinetine (0.5 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) for two to three days. The overnight culture of A. tumefaciens GV3101, transformed with the selected construct, was resuspended with MS20 liquid medium with 100  $ \mu $ M acetosyringone and gently shaken in the dark for 1 h. The stem segments were co-cultured with prepared A. tumefaciens suspension for 1 h, drained on sterilized filter paper and placed on MS20 medium with zeatin (1 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) and 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA; 2 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) for two days. The root segments were then transferred to MS20 medium with zeatin (2 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ), NAA (0.01 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ), kanamycin (50 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) and timentin (300 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ), and the transgenic seedlings should appear after four to five weeks. The generated potato seedlings were then transferred to MS20 medium with kanamycin (50 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ) and timentin (300 mg l $ ^{-1} $ ). Transgenic potato plants with appropriate antibiotic resistance genes (nptII) should root normally and can be transplanted to the growth chamber. Transgenic potato lines were verified using PCR (Supplementary Fig. 16).



### P. infestans isolate culture and DLAs

P. infestans isolates were cultured on rye agar (RSA) plates ten days after zoosprogenesis, as described previously $ ^{98} $ . DLAs were performed on potato lines grown in a growth chamber in the described conditions $ ^{98} $ . Three independent potato leaves from four-to-five-week-old plants were detached and infected with a 10- $ \mu $ l droplet of zoospore suspension (50,000 zoospores per ml). Potato leaves were then placed in the tissue-culture condition (21 $ ^{\circ} $ C, 16 h of light and 8 h of dark) and results were scored five days after zoospore inoculation.

## Protein expression and purification

The HMA domain of Rpi-brk1 (residues 1114–1168; HMA $ ^{brk1} $ ) fused with an N-terminal 6×His-MBP tag and a TEV cleavage site were cloned into pET28a vector, and AVRbrk1 (residues 72–332, PITG_20616 excluded signal peptide and RxLR-DEER motif) fused with an N-terminal 10×His-SUMO tag and a TEV cleavage site were cloned into pET32a vector. Both constructs were expressed in E. coli Rosetta 2 (DE3) pLysS cells (Novagen). Proteins were overexpressed in Rosetta 2 (DE3) pLysS at 16°C for 18–20 h induced by the addition of 0.2 mM IPTG to terrific broth culture medium with an optical density at 600 nm (OD $ _{600nm} $ ) of 0.6–0.8. Collected cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM TCEP and 0.2 mM PMSF) and lysed by D-3L high-pressure homogenizer (PhD-Tech). The supernatants collected by centrifugation at 18,000 rpm for 20 min were loaded onto nickel-chelating sepharose resin (Genscript) and step-eluted with elution buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 250 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP and 500 mM imidazole). The eluted factions were concentrated by a second round of gel filtration chromatography (Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva) with running buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 200 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol and 0.1 mM TCEP). The peak fractions for 6×His-MBP–HMA $ ^{brk1} $  validated by SDS–PAGE were concentrated to 0.8–1 mg ml $ ^{-1} $  by an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter (Millipore). The peak fractions for 10×His-SUMO–AVRbrk1 validated by SDS–PAGE were concentrated to 0.3–0.5 mg ml $ ^{-1} $  by an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter (Millipore). Protein concentrations were determined in triplicate on a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer.

## Luciferase complementation assays

The luciferase complementation assays were performed as described previously $ ^{42} $ . The HMA domain of Rpi-brk1 (HMA $ ^{brk1} $ ) and derived mutants were fused with a C-terminal MBP tag and individually inserted into pCAMBIA1300-cLUC, whereas the PITG_20616 (AVRbrk1, without a signal peptide) was inserted into pCAMBIA1300-nLUC. All plasmid constructs were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and leaves of six-week-old N. benthamiana plants were used for agro-infiltration. N. benthamiana leaves were collected at 48 h after agro-infiltration and then infiltrated with 1 mM luciferin substrate, and a Tanon-5200 Multi Chemiluminescent Imaging System was used for capturing luciferase images.

## Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Proteins for co-immunoprecipitation were fused with a 10×Myc tag or GFP tag by inserting the corresponding coding sequences into the constructs listed in Supplementary Table 19, using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (C115-01, Vazyme Biotech). Fusion proteins were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves by agro-infiltration. After 72 h, infiltrated leaves were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder. Total proteins were extracted from the ground tissues in IP buffer (10% (v/v) glycerol, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 2% (w/v) PVPP, 10 mM DTT, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20). The protein extracts were incubated with Myc-Trap_A beads (ChromoTek) for 3 h at 4 °C. After three washes with washing buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% (v/v) glycerol and 0.1% Tween 20), the immunoprecipitates were eluted in SDS sample buffer. GFP-tagged and Myc-tagged proteins were detected by immunoblotting with anti-GFP (1:5,000, Abmart, M20004L) and anti-c-Myc antibody (1:5,000, Abmart, M20002L) conjugated to IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (LICORbio, dilution 1:20,000), respectively.

## Gel filtration analysis

Analytical size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed at 4 °C with a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated in elution buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol and 0.1 mM TCEP). The 6×His-MBP-HMA $ _{BrK1} $  and 10×His-SUMO-AVR $ _{BrK1} $  proteins were co-incubated in the incubation buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 160 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM TCEP and 0.05% DDM) at 4 °C for 2 h. After protein samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, a 1-ml injection volume of the sample was collected for SEC analysis followed by the SDS-PAGE validation procedure.

## Prediction of the protein interaction complex

The individual structure models for Rpi-brk1 or AVRbrk1 were predicted by the Alphafold3 online server $ ^{99} $ . Then the interaction complexes for Rpi-brk1 and AVRbrk1 were predicted using the docking method by the online server ClusPro (v.2.0) by inputting the achieved structural models $ ^{53} $ . Visual representation, assessment of the complex interactions and measurements of hydrogen-bond distances between the interacting amino acid residues of the interaction interface were presented by UCSF ChimeraX v1.9 (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax/).

## TIR-domain enzymatic analysis

N. benthamiana leaves were collected two days after agro-infiltration, and a 100-mg sample of each treatment was thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen. The potential TIR-domain enzymatic catalytic products in the sample were extracted using an 80% methanol solution at 4 °C. After 2 h of extraction, the sample was centrifuged twice at 15,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for LC-MS analysis. By comparing the designed NLR gene and effector expression combinations with standard samples, their ability to activate the substrate catalytic activity of the TIR domain was assessed.

## BN-PAGE assays

N. benthamiana leaves were collected 36–48 h post-agro-infiltration. Three leaf discs with a diameter of 6 mm were ground thoroughly in liquid nitrogen. Total protein was vortexed for 4–5 s and then extracted in sample buffer (1× NativePAGE Sample Buffer, 1% DDM, BN2008, Invitrogen, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, HY-K0010, MCE) for 10 min at 4 °C. Then the crude protein extract was centrifuged twice at 15,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. To 20 μl of the supernatant sample, 1 μl of 5% G-250 Sample Additive (BN2004, Invitrogen) was added and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was then loaded onto 3–12% Bis-Tris gels (BN1001, Invitrogen). Electrophoresis was first performed at 120 V for 45 min in a 5% native cathode additive running buffer, after which the buffer was replaced with 1% native cathode additive running buffer, and electrophoresis continued at 120 V for 3 h. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was fixed using 20 ml of 8% glacial acetic acid for 15 min, then rinsed with ddH₂O and subsequently subjected to conventional western blot analysis to detect the protein bands.
